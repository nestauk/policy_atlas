Mini dataset, 2026-10-05: search recall per experiment, by source and by review

**What this measures.** Each of fifteen published evidence reviews gives one search text (its *intent*) and a list of the studies it cites (its *target*). Every experiment sends the intent to one search service, keeps the top results, and we count how many of the target studies came back. **Recall** is that share: found studies divided by target studies, averaged over reviews. Only studies with a DOI (Digital Object Identifier) can be matched, so grey literature such as government reports never counts, for any arm.

**The experiments.** The *baselines* are one plain request per review to one service, no language model: `openalex-raw` (OpenAlex's own search), `semantic-scholar` (Semantic Scholar's keyword search, which needs every word to match), `semantic-scholar-snippet` (Semantic Scholar's semantic search, ranked by meaning) and `consensus` (a paid service). `cap50` to `cap1000` is how many of the service's ranked results were kept. The two *pipeline* rows are Policy Atlas's own search stage: `rapid` has a language model write about eighteen queries for OpenAlex and Overton and keeps 50 candidates per backend; `standard` adds abstract screening and a second search round with 100 per backend. Compare a pipeline row with a baseline row of similar size: rapid against cap 50 or 100, standard against cap 100 or 200.

**The data.** Langfuse runs labelled `mini-q-2026-10-05` on dataset `retrieval-ground-truth-mini`: the four gap-map rows (3ie, YEF) carry question-shaped intents, the other eleven their review titles. The `standard` run under that label covers only the four gap-map rows, so its figures splice those four with the eleven `standard` scores kept from the earlier label `mini-2026-10-05`; every other experiment is one run over all fifteen. **Read the sources apart:** the Campbell and SR4ALL rows are whole reference lists that nobody has labelled, and about half of a reference list is background and methods citations no topic search should find, so their recall has a ceiling near 50%. The 3ie and YEF rows (studies coded into a gap map by its screeners) and the four hand-made reviews are labelled, with no such ceiling. Generated by `2026-10-05-mini-question-intents.py`; re-run it to re-derive every number.

1. Total recall per experiment

One request to a semantic search engine beats the whole pipeline. Semantic Scholar's snippet search (free) and Consensus (about 50 cents a review) tie at 11.7% when a thousand results are kept, against 4.8% for the pipeline's standard depth (about $1 a review) and 2.2% for rapid. Even at the same number of results kept the gap holds: at cap 100 the two semantic engines find 5.7% and 7.3% where rapid finds 2.2% with its 100 candidates. Semantic Scholar's keyword search barely registers, because a title-length text rarely has every word present in a paper.

2. Recall per experiment, by source

The order of the engines is the same within every source, but the level differs a lot by source. The 3ie and YEF gap-map rows stay near zero for every engine and for the pipeline, even after their intents were rewritten as questions, which points at the corpus (these are development and youth-justice studies, thinly indexed by scholarly services) rather than at the wording. The four hand-made reviews score highest on both semantic engines. The Campbell and SR4ALL columns look low partly because of the 50% ceiling explained above: 12% to 14% there means roughly a quarter of what could be found.

3. Recall per review

Review by review the two semantic engines often disagree, which suggests a pipeline that queried both would find more than either alone: Consensus finds 34 of the 84 target studies on child sleep and obesity where the snippet engine finds 18 and OpenAlex none, while the snippet engine finds 27 of 84 on loneliness where Consensus finds 16. The pipeline beats neither engine on any review and finds at most two studies at rapid depth on nine of the fifteen. Its one strong result is parental leave at standard depth (13 of 52), the review whose intent reads most like a research question.
