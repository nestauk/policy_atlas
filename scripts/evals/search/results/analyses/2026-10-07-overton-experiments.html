What Overton adds to the search

OpenAlex is an index of academic papers. Overton is an index of policy documents: strategies, evidence reviews, guidance, consultation responses. Yesterday's experiments took paper search from 3% to 22% recall on OpenAlex. Today asked what Overton can add. Short answer: a little for papers, a lot for finding the key policy documents on a topic, which OpenAlex cannot do at all.

The main results

  1. Overton can find academic research papers, through the policy documents that cite them. Every policy document on Overton lists the papers it cites. Search for 100 policy documents on a question, collect the papers they cite, and rank those papers by how many of the 100 cite them. That finds 7.7% of a review's reference list in the first 200 candidates. A plain OpenAlex search finds 3.1%.
  2. But adding those research papers to the OpenAlex search gains almost nothing. OpenAlex alone reaches 22.4% at 200 candidates. Merged with Overton, the best of fourteen ranking rules reaches 23.3%. The papers only Overton finds are each cited by a single policy document, among eleven thousand other such papers, so no rule can lift them into the top 200.
  3. For policy documents, Overton's own citation graph finds the key ones. Policy documents cite other policy documents. Count how many of the search results cite each document, fetch the most-cited ones the search did not return, and the strategies, evidence reviews and guidance on a topic come to the top. On obesity, seven of the top ten were documents the search had never returned.
  4. That ranking beats the search order on a ground truth built from 15 UK strategies. It finds 6.0% of a strategy's cited documents in the first 25, against 3.5% for the search order, and 9.6% against 4.6% when the search is restricted to UK sources.
  5. Most of what Overton returns for UK questions is not from the UK. Only 23% of the results came from UK sources; the Government of Japan supplied more documents than the UK Government and Parliament together. A country filter is now a user option, and the evaluation reports a global and a UK-only number.
  6. Two bugs in the pipeline's Overton calls, recorded for a later fix. Results are sorted by date instead of similarity, and the same document can arrive twice under different sources.

Two indexes, two different graphs

The method that worked yesterday, following citations from the papers already found, depends on what a record cites. On OpenAlex a paper cites papers. On Overton a policy document cites both papers and other policy documents. That one difference gives Overton two uses: a second route to papers, and the only route to key policy documents.

Route 1: finding scholarly papers through Overton

Search Overton for 100 policy documents on the question. Each one lists the papers it cites, with DOIs. Count, for every paper, how many of the 100 documents cite it, and rank by that count. Two calls, no language model, and Overton is a flat subscription so the calls are free.

Route 2: finding the key policy documents

This is what OpenAlex cannot do. Overton's search orders policy documents by similarity to the question, and on every question tried that put committee evidence submissions and consultation responses at the top. The documents an analyst would start from, the strategy, the evidence review, the guidance, were often not in the results at all. They were, however, cited by the results.

So: take the 200 search results. Each lists the policy documents it cites. Count how many of the 200 cite each document. The most-cited ones that the search did not return are fetched by id and added. Rank everything by that count, divided by how widely the document is cited across all of Overton, so that a document cited on every topic does not win on this one.

The similarity damper

The snowball ranks by citations alone, so a document cited by many results on every topic, a carbon budget, a ten-year health plan, an inspectorate's annual report, can sit high on a question it has little to do with. The landmarks it fetches also arrive without any search score, because the search never returned them. The damper fixes both. Each document's title and excerpt, and the question, are turned into embeddings, numeric vectors from the pipeline's embedding model, and the cosine between them, scaled from 0 to 1 across the set, is the document's similarity to the question. The citation score is then multiplied by it:

(citation score + 0.5) × (similarity + 0.2)

The two constants keep a document with zero on one side from vanishing; the rule is the same one that worked on OpenAlex yesterday. A document that many results cite and that is about the question keeps its place. One that many results cite but that is off the question drops: on the three manual questions it removed the 10 Year Health Plan, the Sixth Carbon Budget and Ofsted's annual report from the top 15. The cost is that one call per 100 documents goes to the embedding model, under a cent.

Measuring it

The paper ground truth cannot score policy documents, because it holds none. So a second ground truth was built from Overton itself: 15 UK government strategies on specific topics (smoking, SEND, rough sleeping, heat decarbonisation, gambling and so on), each turned into a question. The target for a strategy is the policy documents it cites; the cutoff is its own date. The measure is the share of those cited documents that appear in the first 25 or 100 documents of each order.

This is a fair way to compare orders and an unfair way to read absolute numbers. Every target is on Overton by construction, a strategy cites selectively, and about half of what it cites (an Act of Parliament, a drug appraisal, a G7 communiqué) is not something a topic search should return. The numbers are low for that reason; the differences between rows are what matter.

Two other signals were tried, how many of the topic's core papers a document cites and which documents cite the core papers; both ranked committee submissions above strategies on most topics and are on hold.

Where the documents come from

The ceiling in that table is the pool, not the ranking: the 300 or so documents fetched for a UK question hold under a tenth of what a UK strategy cites. The composition explains most of it.

What to do next

  1. One small production change for Overton. Sort by relevance. Search with the question and its two paraphrases, 200 documents. Order the policy documents by the citation snowball with the similarity damper. Put the documents' own DOIs and the papers they cite into the paper pool. Remove duplicates by title. Offer the country filter as a user option and report a global and a UK-only measure.
  2. Stop working on the paper cut. Nothing today moved it beyond a point. The extra papers Overton finds are only worth having if the pipeline can screen a much larger pool, which is a budget question.
  3. Measure policy documents where it matters. Add the grey-literature references of the four hand-made reviews to the ground truth. Build a small international target set so the global measure has an instrument that does not favour UK sources.
  4. Three cheap things to try. Do not let the similarity damper promote a document that only one result cites: apply it to documents cited by two or more results, and leave the rest in plain citation order. Rank the long tail of once-cited documents by title similarity, since the titles come with the record and need no calls. Filter by document type before using the "cites the core papers" signal.

Two bugs found on the way

Both are recorded for a production slice; the R&D work does not touch the backend. The pipeline's Overton calls send no sort order, and Overton defaults to newest first, so the 50 documents kept per query today are the newest above a similarity floor rather than the most similar. The same route scored 1.7% in that order against 7.7% by relevance. And the same document can arrive twice under different sources, once as "government" and once as "think tank", so candidates need de-duplicating by title.
