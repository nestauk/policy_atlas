From 3% to 22%: what the search experiments found

One day of experiments on how Policy Atlas finds academic papers for a research question. Two changes did most of the work, both free: following citations from the papers we already found, and asking OpenAlex's semantic search alongside the keyword search. Prompt wording and model choice barely mattered.

How recall is measured

The ground truth is 15 systematic reviews and evidence gap maps in social policy, health and education. Each one comes with the list of studies it cites. We turn the review's title into a research question, run our search with a date cutoff just before the review was published, keep the first 200 candidates, and count how many of the review's references we found. That share is recall. Two runs of the same configuration differ by about one point, so a difference under one point means nothing.

Where the research question comes from

No one writes the question by hand and no language model rewrites it. For a published review, the question is the review's title with its trailing review-type clause removed by a fixed rule: "Risk and protective factors of drug abuse among adolescents: a systematic review" becomes Risk and protective factors of drug abuse among adolescents. The assumption is that a review's title states its scope. For an evidence gap map there is no single title, so the question is assembled from the map's own intervention and outcome labels in one fixed form: What is the evidence on civic and legal education in relation to human rights? The cutoff date is one month before the review was published, so the review itself and anything published alongside it cannot be found.

This matters for reading the numbers in two ways. The question is title-length, which is shorter and blunter than what a user types into Policy Atlas, and it bypasses the app's planner, which normally refines the user's words before search. So these recall figures measure the search stages on their own, with a fixed, slightly unfavourable input.

The ground truth has a known ceiling. Nine of the 15 lists are unlabelled reference lists where about half the entries are background and methods citations that no topic search can find. Five of the 15 reviews turned out to be unreachable by any citation method at all. So the mean of 22% is closer to "about 40% on the topics where this works, near zero on the rest".

The problem: keyword search never returns most of the papers

The missed papers are not sitting further down the result list. For a plain query, recall is 3.1% at 200 results and only 5.1% at 1,000. Every ground-truth paper exists in OpenAlex; the search simply does not return them, because they use different words from the question, or older terminology.

Approach 1: the reference-frequency snowball

Every OpenAlex record carries its reference list. So take the 200 keyword results as seeds, read all their reference lists, and count how many seeds cite each paper. A paper cited by 60 of the 200 seeds is a landmark of the topic, found without anyone knowing its title or its vocabulary. Add the 200 most-cited papers to the candidate set. Cost: eight free OpenAlex calls, about five seconds, no language model.

On the 15 reviews the snowball lifted recall at 200 candidates from 7.0% (keyword seeds alone) to 15.2%. On the most-cited tenth of each labelled review's list, the seminal papers, recall went from 7% to 42%. The parameter sweep showed that adding more cited papers keeps paying, while adding more seeds hurts: results beyond the first 200 are mostly off topic and add noise to the count. In production the seeds should be the papers that passed screening.

Two reviews got worse under any citation ranking. In one, the snowball surfaced the field's classic papers, which the review's list of included primary studies does not contain, so the metric counts them as misses. In the other, the hits were studies from 2020 to 2022 that nothing cites yet. Citations point backwards in time. That second gap is what approach 2 is for.

Approach 2: forward citation chasing

Take the 20 seeds with the most in-set citations, the landmarks among the seeds, and fetch the papers that cite them, using OpenAlex's cites: filter. Score each citing paper by coupling: how many of the 200 seeds it cites, read from its own reference list. Add the top 200. This reaches the newer work that builds on the seeds, exactly where the backward snowball is blind. Cost: ten free OpenAlex calls.

Ranking the citing papers mattered more than which seeds to chase. The fetched sets held 141 ground-truth papers across the 15 reviews. The service's default order ranked 26 of them into the top 200, plain coupling 55, and coupling with each cited seed weighted by its own in-set citations 66. Adding the question words as a search filter on the citing papers was the worst option, and sorting the fetch by citation count did not help. On the same seeds, the forward stage took recall at 200 from 13.6% to 16.3%, and at 400 from 16.8% to 21.1%.

Approach 3: semantic seeds

OpenAlex has an embedding search over titles and abstracts, 50 results per call at a tenth of a cent. We send it eight texts we already have: the question, the two natural-language paraphrases the production prompt writes, and its five keyword queries. The results are merged with the keyword results, round robin, and the first 200 become the seeds.

How the final list is ranked

Every candidate, whether seed, snowball or forward paper, gets one score and the list is sorted by it before the cut:

specificity = (in-set citations + coupling) / log10(citations as of the cutoff + 10)

In-set citations is how many seeds cite the paper, the backward signal. Coupling is how many seeds the paper cites, the forward signal. The denominator damps papers that everyone cites. Citation counts are taken as of the review's cutoff date, so the evaluation does not use citations the review's authors could not have seen. A landmark cited by 60 seeds with 2,400 citations scores 18; the PRISMA guideline cited by 30 seeds with 83,000 citations scores 6; a new paper citing 13 seeds with 6 citations scores 11.

Results

What did not work

Fourteen configurations of prompt, model and reasoning effort for the query-generation step all landed between 14% and 16% with the snowball at 200 candidates, inside two noise floors. Several other ideas added nothing.

Systematic-review-style promptsSynonym blocks, two-concept AND structure, five named query roles. On gpt-5.4-mini they lost three to six points. OpenAlex returns 200 results per query ranked by relevance, and a wide net's first 200 are generic. Short specific phrases win under a cap.
Lay vocabularyThe "alternative vocabulary" query role scored 14 hits across 15 reviews, the lowest of any position. Abstracts use the technical term.
Bigger models for queriesgpt-5.6-luna and gpt-5.6-terra gave better seeds with a boolean prompt but broke OpenAlex's 1,500-character limit, and with the snowball every model landed in the same band. Reasoning effort changed nothing.
Concept blocks composed in codeThe model lists concepts, the code builds the boolean queries. 9.1% at 200, the worst configuration tried.
Sorting by citation countZero new ground-truth papers on every review.
Weighting review-type seedsReview papers have longer reference lists but the same density of ground truth per reference. No gain.
Topic fenceRerunning the queries restricted to the seeds' top three OpenAlex topics added 700 candidates and about one hit per review. Dropped.
Second hop and Adamic-AdarSnowballing again from the snowball reached 3% of misses. Weighting citing seeds by reference-list length was a wash.
Embedding re-rankMultiplying the graph score by the similarity of each candidate to the question added about two points on a 1,900-candidate pool. Real but small; kept because it is free.

What it costs

Measured on our OpenAlex account, reading the cost header on each response: a keyword search page costs $0.0001 whatever its size, a list or filter call $0.0001, a semantic search call $0.001. The best configuration makes 15 keyword calls, 8 semantic calls and about 19 list calls, so about $0.011 per search, plus one query-generation call at about a tenth of a cent. The Member plan allows $20 a day, roughly 1,800 searches. Screening 200 candidates with a language model costs about $2, so the screen, not retrieval, is now the limiting cost.

The recommended configuration

  1. Generate five keyword queries with the production prompt on gpt-5.4-mini or gpt-5.6-luna. One language-model call.
  2. Send each query with its systematic-review and randomised-trial variants to OpenAlex keyword search, 15 calls, and send the question, the two paraphrases and the five queries to OpenAlex semantic search, 8 calls. Merge round robin; the first 200 are the seeds. In production, use the screened-in set as seeds for the later stages.
  3. Read the seeds' reference lists, count how many seeds cite each paper, add the 200 most-cited.
  4. Take the 20 seeds with the most in-set citations, fetch the papers that cite them, score each by how many seeds it cites, add the top 200.
  5. Rank the union by specificity. Hold the first 50 keyword results in their own order as a relevance reserve for recent studies that nothing cites yet.
  6. Screen the first 200, or 400 if the budget allows.
The relevance reserve in step 5 is proposed for production and is not in the experiment script. None of the numbers on this page include it.

Checks and caveats

An independent read-only review found six issues, all fixed or recorded. The main one: the ranking had used today's citation counts in an evaluation with a historical cutoff. Counts are now taken as of each review's cutoff from OpenAlex's per-year data. Re-running the three best configurations moved no number by more than 0.7 points. Three cache keys were made complete, forward paging now stops on the server's reported page size, and self-checks cover every new code path.

Remaining limits: 15 reviews, one run per configuration, a noise floor of about one point, nine unlabelled reference lists with a recall ceiling near 50%, and a mean that hides the split between reachable and unreachable topics. The full 100-review set is the next measurement, and the ground truth would benefit from a cheap labelling pass so recall is measured against on-topic studies only.
