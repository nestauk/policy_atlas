How search and screening work

This page explains what the app does when a user asks it to find evidence for a research question, and how it then decides which of the documents it found are worth keeping. In short: the question is turned into a handful of database searches; the papers those searches return are used to find more papers through their citations; the whole pool is ordered so that the most relevant, the most cited and the most recent papers come first; the top of the list is kept, more or less of it depending on the scope the user chose; and a language model then reads each kept document and says whether it is relevant. Every number on this page is either read from the code or measured on a test set of published reviews; the measured ones say when they were measured.

Diagram A: the search pass

Every search follows the diagram below once, whatever the scope. It uses two databases. OpenAlex is a free index of about 250 million academic works. It offers a keyword search and a meaning-based search (one that matches on what a sentence means rather than the words in it), and every record carries the paper's reference list and the number of times it has been cited. Overton is a subscription index of policy documents (government reports, think-tank papers and the like). It offers a meaning-based search that takes a whole sentence, and every record lists the other policy documents it cites.

The two databases are searched at the same time, and within the OpenAlex side up to 8 requests go out together. Requests to each database are spaced out, and a "too many requests" reply pauses that database for a second. This is why a search takes about 45 seconds at every scope, where the earlier version took about 130. The call counts in the diagram are for a plan with one set of filters; a plan that names more than 100 author countries has to split its keyword searches in two, which doubles those calls.

Keeping the top of each list

The search ends with two ordered lists, one of papers and one of policy documents, and a limit for each. The app walks down each list, skips any document the task already holds (matched by record id, DOI or identical text), stops at the limit, and stores only what it kept. Every record a call returned is accounted for exactly once, as kept, already held, unusable (no title) or over the limit, so the counts add up to the number returned. Each kept document records which call found it: a generated query, one of its narrowed variants, the meaning-based search, a backward or forward citation lookup, or a landmark fetch.

The three scopes are three pairs of caps

100 + 50
Focused. Papers and policy documents kept; about 150 documents screened.
200 + 100
Broad. About 300 documents screened.
400 + 200
Broadest. About 600 documents screened.

Why the scopes differ only in how much they keep, not in how they search: in the design experiments every extra 100 papers kept raised recall by 2 to 5 points, with no point at which it stopped paying, and screening is now cheap enough to read the larger pool. The policy limits are half the paper limits because the policy side found 90% of everything it would ever find within 50 documents and 97% within 100. One method for all three scopes is also easier to test: one search, one measurement, three pairs of numbers. The scope hint in the app says exactly this: "Focused: up to 100 papers and 50 policy documents".

Where the plan's filters go

A plan can carry filters: publication dates, Sustainable Development Goals, document types, languages, open-access status and author countries for papers; publisher type, country, region, source and language for policy documents. A filter is never relaxed. Where each one is applied:

  • Keyword searches: every filter is sent to the database with the query, so only matching records come back. The same for the policy searches.
  • Meaning-based searches: OpenAlex's meaning-based search accepts only the document type, language and retraction filters, plus a year range. So a date range is sent as whole years and the exact dates are checked by the app on the records that come back; the Sustainable Development Goal, open-access and author-country filters are checked by the app in the same way. The search record lists which filters were checked by the app rather than the database.
  • Citation lookups: every filter is sent except the author-country one, which the app checks on the papers that come back. A paper with no affiliation data fails that check and is dropped: when in doubt, the filter wins.
  • Landmarks: a plan's country filter on policy documents applies to the fetched landmarks as it does to the search results.

Each call records how many of its results the app's own checks removed.

Diagram B: screening, stage 1

Every kept document goes to the title-and-abstract screen. The model (gpt-5.6-luna, prompt screen_v4) is given the document's title and abstract, or the title alone when there is no abstract, together with the research question and any inclusion criteria from the plan. It answers relevant, not relevant or unsure, with a confidence. One call is usually enough: a second call is made only when the first says not relevant, so that a single hasty "no" does not lose a relevant document.

What it finds and what it costs

The search is measured against 15 published systematic reviews. For each one, the app is given the review's question and must find the papers the review included; recall is the share it found. scripts/evals/search/checks/production_recall.py runs the real search and screen on those reviews and reports recall, documents, cost and time per review. Targets. The task set a minimum search recall of 12.5% / 17.3% / 24.6% at Focused / Broad / Broadest (what the design experiments reached, less 3 points for noise), and required the screen to keep at least 90% of what the search found. All three are met: before the ordering change of 12 October the figures were 12.9% / 17.8% / 22.3%, with Broadest 2.3 points short. The screen keeps 96% to 97% of what the search found at every scope. One database call failed in the 12 October run, a policy-document search on the human-rights review, so that review's policy side is an undercount. For comparison, the search before this work found 5.0% at Broad on the same reviews (scripts/evals/search/results/history.md, 5 October 2026). Cost note: the dollar figures use the model's list price. The monitoring tool reports five times these amounts because its price entry for the model is five times the list price.

The policy side cannot be measured this way, because published reviews do not list the policy documents they drew on. Instead it is pinned by tests that replay recorded Overton responses for three questions (backend/tests/data/policy_replay/) and check the order of results, the removal of repeated titles, the 15 landmarks, the ordering score, a UK-only filter and the limit.

What it replaced

Before this work (ADR 0012), a Focused search was the 15 keyword searches and 3 policy searches, merged and cut at 50 documents per database, with no citation lookups. Broad and Broadest then repeated search and screen up to three times, each round choosing among four strategies (rewriting the queries from the documents the screen had kept, a small citation lookup, papers suggested by a language model, and a reserve of varied results) and stopping by a rule of its own. The screen asked the model three times per document and took a majority. The policy search returned documents newest first rather than best match first, because no sort order was being requested.

None of the four strategies had been measured on its own. The whole three-round Broadest found 15.3% of the reference lists on four reviews; one pass of the method above found 20.3% on fifteen reviews at the same pool size. The old loop is deleted; its last working version is tagged search-round-loop-last, with the restore recipe in ADR 0038. Old search records keep their old vocabulary and remain readable.
