How search and screening work

This page explains what the app does when a user asks it to find evidence for a research question, and how it then decides which of the documents it found are worth keeping. In short: the question is turned into a handful of database searches; the papers those searches return are used to find more papers through their citations; the whole pool is ordered so that the most relevant, the most cited and the most recent papers come first; the top of the list is kept, more or less of it depending on the scope the user chose; and a language model then reads each kept document and says whether it is relevant.

Diagram A1: the search pass

Every search follows the diagram below once, whatever the scope. Diagrams A2 to A4 then open up its three middle steps: the citation chasing, the policy landmarks, and the ordering of the paper pool. It uses two databases. OpenAlex is a free index of about 400+ million academic works. It offers a keyword search and a meaning-based search (one that matches on what a sentence means rather than the words in it), and every record carries the paper's reference list and the number of times it has been cited. Overton is a subscription index of policy documents (government reports, think-tank papers and the like). It offers a meaning-based search that takes a whole sentence, and every record lists the other policy documents it cites.

The two databases are searched at the same time, and within the OpenAlex side up to 8 requests go out together. Requests to each database are spaced out, and a "too many requests" reply pauses that database for a second. This is why a search takes about 45 seconds at every scope, where the earlier version took about 130. The call counts in the diagram are for a plan with one set of filters; a plan that names more than 100 author countries has to split its keyword searches in two, which doubles those calls.

Diagram A2: citation chasing

The 23 database searches give 200 seed papers. A search matches words or meaning, so it misses papers that describe the same thing in other vocabulary. Citations do not care about vocabulary: a paper that many seeds cite is on the topic whatever it is called, and so is a paper that cites several seeds. The search therefore follows citations from the seeds in both directions. Both directions are plain database lookups and cost no language-model calls.

Backward: the papers the seeds cite. Every OpenAlex record carries the paper's reference list. The app counts, over the 200 seeds, how many seeds cite each work. That count is the work's in-set citations (inset in the code), and the seeds themselves get their count from the same tally. The works with the highest count that are not already seeds, up to 200 of them, are fetched by id, 50 per call. A reference that several seeds share is a much stronger signal than one that a single seed holds, and the count follows the paper into the ordering.

Forward: the papers that cite the seeds. Fetching everything that cites all 200 seeds would be far too much, and one seed with thousands of citations would flood the pool with its citers. So the app chases only 20 seeds: the ones other seeds cite most, skipping any seed cited more than 300 times in all. It fetches the papers that cite any of the 20, in pages of 200, up to 10 pages. Each citing paper is scored by coupling, the number of distinct seeds it cites, and by a weighted form in which each cited seed counts log2(1 + that seed's in-set citations). Citing a seed the set itself relies on is worth more than citing a peripheral one. The 200 citing papers with the highest weighted coupling join the pool.

Diagram A3: policy documents and landmarks

The policy side is simpler. Overton has no "cited by" lookup, and its search takes a whole sentence, so three meaning-based searches go out: the question itself and its two paraphrases, each asking for best-match order and taking up to 134 documents (three pages of 50, the last page cut so that the three together stay within 400). The three lists are merged by taking the top of each in turn. Overton often holds the same report twice, once from the publisher and once from an archive, so a title that appears twice, compared after lower-casing and removing punctuation, keeps only its best-ranked copy.

Landmarks. Every Overton record lists the policy documents it cites. Counting those citations across the results shows which documents the result set leans on. A document that many results cite but that the search itself did not return is usually a central report of the field, one whose title did not happen to match any paraphrase. The app calls it a landmark, and fetches the 15 most cited such documents by id, one call each. If the plan filters policy documents by country, a landmark from another country is dropped like any other result. The academic papers that Overton says each document cites are not fetched: the paper pool comes from OpenAlex only.

Ranking. Results and landmarks are ranked together by the same kind of tie score as the papers: citations from the other results, divided by log10(total citations + 10). Equal scores are broken by the raw count of citing results, then by search position. A landmark has no search position, so it comes after a result with the same score. Most results cite nothing else in the set and score 0, so the tail of the list is simply the merged search order. The first 50, 100 or 200 are kept.

Diagram A4: ordering the paper pool

The pool now holds up to 600 papers: 200 seeds, up to 200 that the seeds cite, up to 200 that cite the seeds. Only the first 100, 200 or 400 are kept, so the order decides what the user sees and what the screen reads. First, duplicate copies go. OpenAlex often holds a preprint and the journal version of one paper as two records. Two records with the same normalised title and the same set of author names are one paper, and the more cited copy is kept. A shared title with different authors stays two papers (many reviews have the same title), and a record with no title or no author names is never merged, because there is not enough evidence that it is the same work.

Then the order. One score alone favours one kind of paper. Ordering by tie score put heavily cross-cited classics first and pushed recent work out of the cut; ordering by search rank let a broad query's off-topic results in. So the pool is built from three ordered lists, and the final order takes one paper from each list in turn:

  1. Relevant, by reciprocal rank fusion. This is a standard way to merge several ranked lists without comparing their scores: a paper gets 1 ÷ (60 + its position) from every list that holds it, and the amounts are summed. A paper near the top of several lists scores highest; a paper that one broad query returned at position 150 scores almost nothing. The lists fused are the 23 query result lists plus the pool ordered by weighted coupling (how strongly each paper cites the seeds), and that last list is counted three times, so a paper that cites many seeds is treated as if three queries had returned it near the top. Only papers that some query returned, or that cite at least one seed, have a score and appear in this list.
  2. Established (seminal in the code): every paper in the pool, by in-set citations, then by total citations. The works the seed set leans on most, whether or not any query found them.
  3. Recent (novel): papers published in the last five years, 2021 onward in 2026. Those that have a fusion score come first, in fusion order; the rest follow by tie score.

The final order takes the top paper from the relevant list, then the top of the established list, then the top of the recent list, and round again. A paper already placed is skipped, which happens often because a recent paper is usually also a relevant one. When the relevant and recent lists run out, the established list, which holds every paper, supplies the rest, so the tail of the pool is in in-set citation order. The first 100, 200 or 400 are kept, skipping any paper the task already holds. In practice the first 100 hold about a third of each kind. On the 15 test reviews this raised recall at every scope compared with the tie score alone; the figures are in the measured section below.

Keeping the top of each list

The search ends with two ordered lists, one of papers and one of policy documents, and a limit for each. The app walks down each list, skips any document the task already holds (matched by record id, DOI or identical text), stops at the limit, and stores only what it kept. Every record a call returned is accounted for exactly once, as kept, already held, unusable (no title) or over the limit, so the counts add up to the number returned. Each kept document records which call found it: a generated query, one of its narrowed variants, the meaning-based search, a backward or forward citation lookup, or a landmark fetch.

The three scopes are three pairs of caps

100 + 50
Focused. Papers and policy documents kept; about 150 documents screened.
200 + 100
Broad. About 300 documents screened.
400 + 200
Broadest. About 600 documents screened.

Why the scopes differ only in how much they keep, not in how they search: in the design experiments every extra 100 papers kept raised recall by 2 to 5 points, with no point at which it stopped paying, and screening is now cheap enough to read the larger pool. The policy limits are half the paper limits because the policy side found 90% of everything it would ever find within 50 documents and 97% within 100. One method for all three scopes is also easier to test: one search, one measurement, three pairs of numbers. The scope hint in the app says exactly this: "Focused: up to 100 papers and 50 policy documents".

Where the plan's filters go

A plan can carry filters: publication dates, Sustainable Development Goals, document types, languages, open-access status and author countries for papers; publisher type, country, region, source and language for policy documents. A filter is never relaxed. Where each one is applied:

  • Keyword searches: every filter is sent to the database with the query, so only matching records come back. The same for the policy searches.
  • Meaning-based searches: OpenAlex's meaning-based search accepts only the document type, language and retraction filters, plus a year range. So a date range is sent as whole years and the exact dates are checked by the app on the records that come back; the Sustainable Development Goal, open-access and author-country filters are checked by the app in the same way. The search record lists which filters were checked by the app rather than the database.
  • Citation lookups: every filter is sent except the author-country one, which the app checks on the papers that come back. A paper with no affiliation data fails that check and is dropped: when in doubt, the filter wins.
  • Landmarks: a plan's country filter on policy documents applies to the fetched landmarks as it does to the search results.

Each call records how many of its results the app's own checks removed.

Diagram B: screening, stage 1

Every kept document goes to the title-and-abstract screen. The model (gpt-5.6-luna, prompt screen_v4) is given the document's title and abstract, or the title alone when there is no abstract, together with the research question and any inclusion criteria from the plan. It answers relevant, not relevant or unsure, with a confidence. One call is usually enough: a second call is made only when the first says not relevant, so that a single hasty "no" does not lose a relevant document.

What it finds and what it costs

The search is measured against 15 published systematic reviews. For each one, the app is given the review's question and must find the papers the review included; recall is the share it found. scripts/evals/search/checks/production_recall.py runs the real search and screen on those reviews and reports recall, documents, cost and time per review. Targets. The task set a minimum search recall of 12.5% / 17.3% / 24.6% at Focused / Broad / Broadest (what the design experiments reached, less 3 points for noise), and required the screen to keep at least 90% of what the search found. All three are met: before the ordering change of 12 October the figures were 12.9% / 17.8% / 22.3%, with Broadest 2.3 points short. The screen keeps 96% to 97% of what the search found at every scope. One database call failed in the 12 October run, a policy-document search on the human-rights review, so that review's policy side is an undercount. For comparison, the search before this work found 5.0% at Broad on the same reviews (scripts/evals/search/results/history.md, 5 October 2026). Cost note: the dollar figures use the model's list price. The monitoring tool reports five times these amounts because its price entry for the model is five times the list price.

The policy side cannot be measured this way, because published reviews do not list the policy documents they drew on. Instead it is pinned by tests that replay recorded Overton responses for three questions (backend/tests/data/policy_replay/) and check the order of results, the removal of repeated titles, the 15 landmarks, the ordering score, a UK-only filter and the limit.

What it replaced

Before this work (ADR 0012), a Focused search was the 15 keyword searches and 3 policy searches, merged and cut at 50 documents per database, with no citation lookups. Broad and Broadest then repeated search and screen up to three times, each round choosing among four strategies (rewriting the queries from the documents the screen had kept, a small citation lookup, papers suggested by a language model, and a reserve of varied results) and stopping by a rule of its own. The screen asked the model three times per document and took a majority. The policy search returned documents newest first rather than best match first, because no sort order was being requested.

None of the four strategies had been measured on its own. The whole three-round Broadest found 15.3% of the reference lists on four reviews; one pass of the method above found 20.3% on fifteen reviews at the same pool size. The old loop is deleted; its last working version is tagged search-round-loop-last, with the restore recipe in ADR 0038. Old search records keep their old vocabulary and remain readable.
