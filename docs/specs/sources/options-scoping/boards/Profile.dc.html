Youth guarantee

Run a full evidence searchStart a theory of changeSwap on shortlist

Every 16 to 24 year old who leaves education or work gets a guaranteed offer of a job, apprenticeship, training or continued education within four months. It creates an entitlement, and puts the burden of supplying offers on local providers.

At a glance

  • How big, as reported: six of nine country evaluations found NEET rate reductions of one to three points. Effects on sustained employment are rarely measured.
  • How sure: moderate. Nine studies, none randomised.
  • Where tried: EU-wide since 2013; Finland, Austria, Portugal, Italy, France, Spain and Sweden evaluated.
  • Transferability: conditional. Two of the five support factors are unknown for the UK because the plan does not say.
  • Key assumption, test first: local providers can supply enough quality offers within four months. Load-bearing, and the evidence for it is weak.

How it works

Collapse −

Registering as NEET starts a clock. Within four months the local service must make a concrete offer. That forces early contact, and it puts a supply obligation on providers rather than a search obligation on the young person. (inference from 6 sources)

Main failure mode. Providers meet the deadline with low-quality offers. The young person returns to NEET within months, the headline rate improves, and time spent NEET does not. (observed in Italy and Spain)

What it is made of

Collapse −
  • A single registration point for NEET young people (7 documents)
  • An offer deadline of four months from registration (9 documents)
  • Ring-fenced funding to local providers per registered young person (5 documents)
  • Follow-up contact at three and six months after an offer is taken (2 documents, thin)

Evidence for and against

Collapse −

Six of nine evaluations found reductions in the NEET rate. Three found none. No evaluation measures employment beyond twelve months after an offer.

Strongest for. Finland: the NEET rate fell 2.8 points over four years where registration reach passed 80% (Eurofound 2021, quasi-experimental). Austria: 1.9 points, the only state with a quality floor on offers (Escudero and Mourelo 2017, panel). Across countries, reductions of one to three points in six of nine states within three years, "concentrated where implementation was funded" (Eurofound 2021).

Strongest against. Italy: no measurable change in five years; offers were mostly short training courses (Pastore 2019, difference-in-differences). Spain: the rate fell with the labour market, not with the guarantee (Cabases 2020, panel). No study follows employment past twelve months.

Common criticisms. It displaces existing youth services rather than adding capacity. The headline rate can be met with low-quality offers. Reach is lowest among the inactive, the group driving the UK rise.

How it varies in practice

Collapse −
  • Deadline. Four months in most states; six in Italy and Spain, where results were weakest.
  • Age range. 15 to 24 originally; extended to 29 in 2020 by most states.
  • Offer quality. Only Austria and Finland set a minimum duration and pay for offers.
  • Funding route. Per-registration grants to municipalities in Finland; national tenders in Italy.

Case studies

Collapse −

Finland, Ohjaamo one-stop centres. From 2015, municipalities ran walk-in centres combining employment, health and housing advice, with registration within days of leaving education. Reach passed 80% and the NEET rate fell 2.8 points in four years. It worked because funding followed each registration and existing youth workers were redeployed rather than new staff hired. Effects were smaller in rural municipalities without a centre. (academic: Eurofound 2021, Savolainen 2020)

Austria, AusBildung bis 18. From 2016 the guarantee was paired with a training duty to age 18 and a quality floor: offers must be paid or lead to a qualification. The NEET rate fell 1.9 points and early leaving from training fell by a third. The quality floor and existing coaching services made the difference. It relies on a duty on the young person, which your plan excludes. (grey literature: Escudero and Mourelo 2017, BMAW 2022)

Italy, Garanzia Giovani. From 2014 to 2020, online registration with providers paid per placement through national tenders. Take-up was low and the NEET rate did not measurably change in five years. The deadline was six months, offers were mostly short courses, there was no quality standard, and regions with weak services delivered least. This is the pattern the UK would repeat without funded local capacity. (academic: Pastore 2019)

Portugal, Garantia Jovem. Registration through employment offices, with offers weighted toward traineeships. The source is an implementation account only and reports no outcomes, so it does not count toward evidence strength. (web, verified link: IEFP programme page)

Assumptions

Collapse −
  • Local providers can supply enough quality offers within four months. Load-bearing; evidence weak. This is the one to test first.
  • Young people who are inactive will register. Load-bearing; evidence weak.
  • An offer taken up leads to sustained education or employment. Not load-bearing; evidence moderate.
  • The guarantee can run without a sanction element. Not load-bearing; untested. Your note: assess without sanctions.

What it would take

Collapse −
  • Implementation. A statutory entitlement or a published policy commitment, a registration route, and commissioning of local providers (Eurofound 2021)
  • Reported costs. €500 to €1,500 per participant-year across evaluated states (Eurofound 2021)
  • Time to impact. Twelve to eighteen months to first offers; rate effects reported from year two (Eurofound 2021)
  • Distributional notes. Reach was lowest among the inactive and those with health conditions in every evaluation that reported it (ILO 2020)
