# The Theory of Change approach

*Synthesised from the HM Treasury Magenta Book (May 2026 update), Nesta/TSIP's "Guidance for Developing a Theory of Change for Your Programme" (Centre for Social Action Innovation Fund), and Policy Atlas Theory of Change user research.*

---

## 1. What a Theory of Change is

A Theory of Change (ToC) is an explicit account of how and why an intervention is expected to bring about a desired change. It sets out the causal chain from what the intervention does through to its long-term results, the assumptions each step depends on, the strength of the evidence behind those assumptions, and the wider context that could help or hinder it.

The Magenta Book treats understanding the intervention as the first step of any evaluation. It expects that understanding to be built by synthesising existing evidence and producing a ToC. A complete ToC covers:

- the problem the intervention addresses and the change it aims to bring about;
- the causal chain of events expected to produce that change;
- the main actors, and the groups expected to be affected;
- the conditions required for success;
- the assumptions made, and how strong or weak the supporting evidence is;
- wider contextual factors, such as other policy changes and economic, social and environmental shifts.

Nesta's guidance adds a useful discipline: a programme ToC should describe impact **from the beneficiary's point of view**. It should not describe the organisation's scale, growth plans or operations. At Nesta, a ToC is a mandatory requirement for Level 1 of the Standards of Evidence.

The Magenta Book also stresses that diagrams and logic models are *representations* of the ToC, not the ToC itself. The theory is the causal reasoning and evidence. The diagram is a way of showing it.

## 2. Why build one

The two sources agree on three core uses.

1. **Designing and stress-testing the intervention.** The process exposes gaps and weak links. It also gives designers and implementers a chance to agree on how the intervention is supposed to work. The Magenta Book recommends starting ToC thinking at the rationale and objectives stage, to sharpen objectives and test early ideas. At appraisal stage, it helps compare how different options are expected to work and how strong the evidence is for each.
2. **Communication.** A ToC explains clearly and convincingly what a programme does and how it creates impact.
3. **Evaluation.** A ToC identifies the outcomes to measure, the areas of uncertainty and the weaknesses in the evidence. These become the evaluation questions. The ToC also guides the choice of evaluation methods, data collection and the monitoring system.

The Magenta Book also lists **identifying risks and dependencies** as a key use. A ToC lets design and implementation be adjusted before problems materialise.

User research participants added a fourth benefit: **team alignment**. Building the ToC is valuable because it forces a shared view. But it is expensive in time and resources when done manually.

## 3. Components and terminology

The Magenta Book's linear example, based on Mayne (2017), uses a chain that civil servants know well. Use it as the default vocabulary.

| Stage | Magenta Book meaning | Nesta guidance equivalent |
|---|---|---|
| **Inputs** | The resources committed and activities undertaken | **Activities**: what the programme does directly for beneficiaries, or what they do as part of it |
| **Outputs** | What is delivered or produced (e.g. numbers going through a programme, applications processed) | (Folded into activities and dosage) |
| **Outcomes** | The early or medium-term results (e.g. employment rates, wages) | **Intermediate outcomes**: all the separate changes in beneficiaries that lead to the ultimate goal |
| **Impact** | The long-term results | **Ultimate goal(s)**: the primary impact on beneficiaries |
| **Assumptions** | Sit between stages: about who takes up the intervention, about early behaviour change, and about benefits and unintended effects | Sit on specific causal links, focused on the most contestable ones |
| **Context** | Contextual factors and external influences that frame the whole chain | Not covered explicitly |

### Terminology notes

- **Outputs versus outcomes.** Outputs are things the intervention *delivers* and can count directly, such as sessions run, grants paid or people reached. Outcomes are *changes* that result, such as in skills, behaviour, wellbeing or employment. Mixing these up is the most common error.
- **Time horizons.** Users in the research found short-, medium- and long-term outcome layers intuitive. This maps cleanly onto outputs, outcomes and impact.
- **Activities and operations.** Nesta's guidance deliberately excludes operational or preparatory activity, such as referral processes, staff training, marketing and programme setup, from the beneficiary-facing ToC. The Magenta Book's "inputs" are broader and include resources committed. When building a programme ToC, keep the main chain focused on what reaches beneficiaries. Record operational inputs separately where an evaluation needs them, for example for cost or process questions.
- **Avoid "mechanism" as a layer label.** In user research, labelling two layers "mechanism" confused participants. Use the familiar inputs/activities, outputs, outcomes and impact terms, with a visible key. Reserve "mechanism" for its evaluation meaning: the *reason* a step leads to the next. In realist evaluation, a mechanism captures people's reasoning and choices when faced with a policy measure.
- **Ultimate goals and impact.** There is a subtle difference here. Nesta advises that ultimate goals should represent the programme's direct impact on beneficiaries, not the more distant societal effects that follow from it. The Magenta Book's "impact" means long-term results more generally. In practice, define the impact as the furthest result you can credibly measure and claim. Treat anything beyond that as context or wider contribution.

## 4. How to build a Theory of Change

### 4.1 Two entry points

Users arrive in two ways, and both need a route.

- **Outcome-first.** The user knows the change they want but not yet how to achieve it. This is backwards mapping: start from the impact and repeatedly ask what must be true just before each step for it to happen, until you reach things an intervention can directly cause. User research participants called this the stronger approach. They wanted an AI that reasons backwards *alongside* them, rather than handing them a list of options.
- **Intervention-first.** The user has a known or proposed intervention and wants to set out and test how it leads to impact. This is forward mapping. It works best when treated as a test of the logic rather than a description of it.

A third mode, **stress-testing an existing ToC**, matters just as much. Many users already have a ToC drawn in PowerPoint, Excel, Miro or on paper, and want to check its links and assumptions against evidence.

### 4.2 Core steps

These steps follow Nesta's five core elements, extended with the Magenta Book's emphasis on evidence and context.

1. **Define the ultimate goal or impact.** Usually one or two goals, rarely more than three. Each must be measurable. Make the target population explicit.
2. **Identify the intermediate outcomes.** These are all the separate changes needed to reach the goal. They must be measurable, and detailed enough to reflect the real complexity of the programme. Some outcomes will lead to other outcomes, not straight to the goal.
3. **Identify the activities, inputs and outputs.** Describe what the intervention does in enough detail that an outsider would understand it. Include the **dosage**: how much each beneficiary receives, such as twelve weekly sessions. This is different from how many beneficiaries are reached.
4. **Draw the causal links.** Show which activities lead to which outcomes, which outcomes lead to which others, and which lead to the goal. Expect some activities to drive several outcomes, and some outcomes to have several causes. Balance completeness with clarity: drop secondary links if they make the diagram hard to follow.
5. **Examine the assumptions.** For each contestable link, state why it might *not* hold in practice. Attach every assumption to a specific link; don't leave assumptions floating on the diagram. Nesta treats this as the final step. The Magenta Book adds that you should assess the **strength of evidence** behind each assumption.
6. **Synthesise the evidence for each link.** The Magenta Book calls evidence synthesis a key part of producing a ToC. Bringing together existing evidence shows where it is weak, and those weak points become the questions an evaluation must answer. Where studies conflict, consider that the intervention may work for some groups in some circumstances but not others.
7. **Consider the negative programme theory and alternatives.** Set out the reasons the causal steps might fail and the outcome might not occur. Also consider alternative explanations for how the outcome could come about. This is essential for theory-based impact evaluation.
8. **Map the context.** Record the external factors and other policies that could affect whether, and how, the outcomes happen.

### 4.3 Process principles

- **Collaborate.** The Magenta Book says the best ToCs come from extensive collaboration with designers, implementers, beneficiaries and interest groups. At a minimum, the ToC should be stress-tested with key stakeholders. Nesta recommends starting as a group, with paper and sticky notes, before moving to software.
- **Use a range of evidence.** This can include research literature, focus groups, expert panels, administrative data and practitioner knowledge.
- **Split large interventions.** A large intervention with many elements can be broken into a series of linked models covering different aspects.
- **Keep it alive.** The ToC should be refined over the lifetime of the intervention and the evaluation as new evidence emerges. The Magenta Book's Test and Learn approach uses rapid, small-scale testing to check the riskiest assumptions and refine the ToC before scaling up.
- **Colour-code it and include a key.** Use distinct colours for goals, outcomes, activities and assumptions.

## 5. Simple and complex Theories of Change

The Magenta Book says ToCs can range from simple descriptions to more complex analyses. A more sophisticated ToC sets out:

- the precise causal mechanisms between steps;
- alternative mechanisms leading to the same outcomes;
- the assumptions and evidence behind each step;
- how contextual, behavioural and organisational factors affect whether outcomes come about.

For complex interventions, a ToC may capture interactions, feedback loops and system boundaries. It names several mapping tools: ToC mapping, logic models, outcomes mapping and system mapping. The right one depends on the intervention, the complexity of the system and the type of evaluation planned. The ToC's structure should also match the evaluation method. For example, realist evaluation needs context–mechanism–outcome statements.

Further detail is in the Magenta Book supplementary guidance, *Handling Complexity in Evaluation*. Where outcomes are uncertain or emerging, it points to developmental evaluation and Test and Learn approaches.

**Product position:** lead with simplicity. Users valued how a simple ToC makes a complex problem navigable. Handle linear, Magenta Book-style ToCs well first. Treat complexity features, such as feedback loops, mediators and systems maps like the obesity map, as a longer-term goal, guided by how the Magenta Book treats complexity.

## 6. Using AI in Theory of Change work

The 2026 Magenta Book explicitly anticipates AI support. It says AI can help visualise the links between stages, and can surface a wider range of potential outcomes, including unintended consequences and long-term impacts. It can also gather evidence from literature and policy documents to identify patterns, gaps and alternative explanations.

It sets clear conditions:

- **The evaluator interprets.** The evaluator's role in interpreting patterns and identifying causal pathways remains critical.
- **Evidence reviews have known weaknesses.** AI-assisted reviews raise problems with systematic coverage, selection bias, paywalled sources, opaque filtering and reproducibility. Outputs need quality assurance, including checking that sources are accurate and actually exist.
- **AI use must be accountable.** It must be proportionate, quality-assured, verifiable and transparently communicated to stakeholders.

These conditions match what user research participants asked for:

- **Auditable evidence.** An easily checked reference list, and clarity on how low, medium and high evidence ratings are derived and where assumptions come from.
- **Clear weak links.** Participants wanted to know whether a link is weak because there is *no evidence* or because there is *evidence against* it.
- **Awareness of skew.** Some noted that evidence tends to be UK-based or generic.
- **A human in the loop.** Evidence-based interpretation is never fully objective, so users should be able to configure evidence searches and steer the result by editing.

## 7. Implications for Policy Atlas

These are the agreed decisions from the user research workshop, set against the guidance above.

- **Match the Magenta Book format.** Use inputs/activities, outputs, outcomes and impact, with assumptions on links and a context band. Consider short-, medium- and long-term outcome layers. Add a visible key.
- **Support both entry points.** Provide outcome-first backwards reasoning with an AI working alongside the user, and an intervention-first route. Consider a triage step that routes users between option scoping and ToC.
- **Support stress-testing and user-built ToCs.** Allow users to upload or recreate an existing ToC and test it. Offer a mode where the user builds the ToC and Policy Atlas finds evidence for each node and link.
- **Make the canvas the default, and make it legible.** Signal that connections are clickable. Explain what line thickness and colour mean through a key and tooltips. Add brief on-canvas instructions.
- **Make the evidence auditable.** Show how ratings are derived and where assumptions come from. Distinguish "no evidence" from "evidence against". Provide a reference list that is easy to check.
- **Fix the chat.** Make it easy to find, offer suggested questions, use plain language, and nudge users when a prompt fails.
- **Export before collaboration.** Prioritise strong content generation and export to tools people already use, such as Google Slides or Miro. Don't build real-time co-editing, which risks recreating Miro. Change tracking and save-and-share remain open options.

## 8. Quality checklist

This combines Nesta's final checklist with the Magenta Book's requirements.

**Structure**
- [ ] Includes activities/inputs, outputs, outcomes, impact or ultimate goals, causal links and assumptions.
- [ ] Each element type is colour-coded and labelled, with a key.
- [ ] Makes the target population clear.
- [ ] States the dosage of activities.
- [ ] Keeps operational detail (training, referrals, marketing, setup) and programme scale out of the main chain.

**Causal logic**
- [ ] All outcomes and goals are measurable.
- [ ] Some intermediate outcomes link to each other, not only straight to the goal.
- [ ] Causal links are visually clear and easy to follow.
- [ ] Every assumption sits on a specific causal link.
- [ ] Alternative explanations and the negative programme theory have been considered.

**Evidence and context**
- [ ] Each assumption and link is graded by strength of evidence, with traceable sources.
- [ ] Evidence gaps have been turned into evaluation questions.
- [ ] Contextual factors and external influences are recorded.

**Process**
- [ ] Developed with, or at least stress-tested by, designers, implementers and affected groups.
- [ ] There is a plan to revise the ToC as evidence emerges.
