Frame 3 · Box panel

Figures on a box: baseline and indicators

Long-term outcome L1

Children eat less excess energy

Children who eat more than they need for healthy growth gain weight over time; the theory expects fewer snacks and fewer purchases to cut this excess.

Figures

Children eat 140 to 500 more calories a day than they need (England, baseline) “children overall eat 140–500 excess kcal per day” Public Health England · 2018 · government evidence review

How it could be measured

Daily energy intake against need (National Diet and Nutrition Survey).

Detailed information

Summary

Goal: fewer children in England living with obesity within five years. If adverts for less healthy food are removed from TV before 9pm and from paid online spaces, then children will see fewer of them, eat fewer advert-prompted snacks and make fewer requests, because food adverts prompt immediate eating and purchase requests.

Population: children under 18 in England · Time horizon: five years · Directly influenced: up to outputs.

Context. Children watch most television outside children's programmes, and food marketing is moving to social media and influencers, which the rules cover only in part.

Weakest critical links. Whether fewer covered adverts mean children see fewer adverts overall (#2: not sure, mixed), and whether fewer requests change what households buy (#4: too little evidence).

How change happens

Fewer adverts, less snacking. Removing covered adverts (A1 → P1) should cut children's exposure (S1). Experiments consistently show children eat more after seeing food adverts, so less exposure should mean fewer advert-prompted snacks (S3) and less excess energy (L1) [1][2].

Fewer requests, different shopping. …

How it could fail. Advertising may move to spaces the rules do not cover (F1), and children may replace lost calories with other food (F2) [4].
