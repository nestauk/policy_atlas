How Policy Atlas weighs the evidence for a connection

Every connection in a theory of change gets one evidence verdict. This page explains how that verdict is made: a simplified version of the Balance Evidence Assessment Method (BEAM), worked through with the food-advertising theory from run 9.

A theory of change is a chain of steps: if this happens, then that follows, because of some mechanism. Each arrow between two steps is an assumption. BEAM, from Christie and colleagues (2023, Conservation Science and Practice), is a method for weighing diverse evidence for or against one such assumption. It pictures the evidence as blocks on a balance: where a block sits says whether the evidence supports or refutes the step, and how big it is says how much that evidence should count.

In one sentence: each document gets a position (how it bears on the step) and a size (its quality times its relevance here); the blocks add up to three numbers; fixed thresholds on those numbers give the verdict. A model reads the documents, but code makes the verdict, so the same evidence always gives the same answer.

The method

From documents to a verdict

The check runs six steps for each connection. Only step 2 asks a language model for a judgement. Every later step is arithmetic in evidence/grade.py, computed each time the page is read, so a verdict can never drift from the judgements behind it.

Step 2: the judge places each document on the balance

The judge reads one document's title and abstract against one connection and answers three things. First, support: where the block sits.

  • +2Strongly supports. A direct test, and the effect moved the way the step says, clearly.
  • +1Weakly supports. Points the same way, but less directly: an association, a small or uncertain effect, a proxy, or an effect in some groups only.
  • 0Mixed. Supports and refutes about equally, or shows no clear effect in a study too small to tell.
  • −2Refutes. No change in a study able to detect one, the opposite change, or the mechanism failing. As in BEAM, there is one refutes category.

A document that does not test the step at all (a commentary, a study of a neighbouring step, a topic match only) is not about it and leaves the balance. Second, relevance from 0 to 3, set by BEAM's test: how many extra assumptions are needed to carry this finding over to this population, in this place, now. Three needs none; one needs several small or a few large leaps, such as another country's media market. A study of a similar intervention that works through the same mechanism counts, usually at relevance 1. Third, the exact quote the answer rests on; code checks it is in the text and drops the answer if not.

Step 3: quality from the study design

BEAM scores information reliability and source reliability separately. Policy Atlas already appraises every study it finds for the Evidence search, on a 1 to 5 scale, so it reuses that one score as quality Q, from 1 to 3:

  • Appraisal 5 or 4 → 3; 3 → 2; 2, 1 or no score → 1.
  • A web page from a publisher not on the trusted list (trusted_publishers.toml) → 1, whatever its score.
  • A modelling study → 2 when the judge called the model robust, else 1.

Step 4: the size of each block

weight = Q × R ÷ 9

So one high-quality, fully relevant study (3 × 3) weighs exactly 1, and a study with relevance 0 disappears. BEAM's own formula is I × S × R ÷ 27; ours has one quality score in place of two.

Step 5: three numbers

Adding up the blocks gives:

  • W, the total weight: how much evidence there is, in units of "one strong, fully relevant study".
  • S̄, the average support: each block's support value weighted by its size, from −2 to +2. Which way the evidence points.
  • R%, the share refuting: the part of W on the refutes side.

Worked through for #4:

Blocks (Q × R)
weak 2×2 = 4 · refutes 2×2 = 4 · strong 2×3 = 6 · strong 3×1 = 3 · weak 3×1 = 3 · strong 3×1 = 3
W
(4 + 4 + 6 + 3 + 3 + 3) ÷ 9 = 23 ÷ 9 = 2.56
S̄
(4×1 − 4×2 + 6×2 + 3×2 + 3×1 + 3×2) ÷ 23 = 23 ÷ 23 = +1.00
R%
4 ÷ 23 = 17%
Verdict
W ≥ 1, S̄ ≥ +1 and R% under 25%: Confident. Under the first rule (refuting share under 15%) this was "Not sure: mixed": one refuting study among five supporting ones outvoted them. That was the case for recalibrating.

The verdict

Six verdicts from two numbers

The rule reads the evidence in two steps. First, is there enough to say anything? Below a total weight of 4/9 (about half of one strong, relevant study) the verdict is Not enough evidence, whatever the documents say: one study from another country cannot decide a step alone. Second, which way does it point, and how clearly? With some evidence (W from 4/9 to 1) the verdict can only lean. With enough (W of 1 or more) it can be Confident, Mixed or Not confident, or lean when the direction is there but not clear-cut.

  • Confident — W ≥ 1, S̄ ≥ +1 and under 25% of the weight refutes. Run 9: 9
  • Leaning supports — S̄ ≥ +0.5, but either W is between 4/9 and 1, or W ≥ 1 and the evidence falls short of Confident. Run 9: 2
  • Mixed — W ≥ 1 and S̄ between −0.5 and +0.5: enough evidence, no clear direction. Run 9: 2
  • Leaning against — S̄ ≤ −0.5, but either W is between 4/9 and 1, or W ≥ 1 and S̄ is above −1. Run 9: 3
  • Not confident: evidence against — W ≥ 1 and S̄ ≤ −1. Run 9: 1
  • Not enough evidence — W under 4/9; or W between 4/9 and 1 with no clear direction. Includes when nothing was found. Run 9: 6

#5 · Leaning against

Two documents, both refuting: W = 0.67, S̄ = −2. Some evidence, all one way, so it leans. But both are poor matches for an online step (a TV study and a study of influencer videos), which is a judging problem, not a rule problem; see the note below.

#8 · Mixed

"Fewer requests, fewer household purchases": four weakly supporting documents and one refuting. W = 2.0, enough evidence, but S̄ = +0.33: it neither clearly supports nor refutes.

From connections to the whole theory

BEAM judges a set of assumptions by its critical weakest link (Salafsky and colleagues, 2022): a chain is only as strong as its weakest essential step. Policy Atlas marks one critical connection with a red warning triangle: of the connections on the kept success pathways, the one with the lowest verdict, from worst to best Not confident, Leaning against, Not enough evidence, Mixed, Leaning supports, Confident. When several tie, it is the one the most pathways pass through. The theory's overall confidence is the lowest verdict among its critical connections, and the report's "weakest critical links" and "what to test" start there.

A flag, close to the threshold, is still computed for each connection: the average support within 0.2 of a threshold, or the total weight near 4/9 or 1. The page no longer shows it as a sentence; the eval reads it.

Honest limits

What the verdict does not do

  • It is only as good as the judging. The rule weighs what the judge says. In run 9, connection #5 (online adverts) was refuted by a TV study, and #1 shows a figure from the next step. These are being fixed in the judge and the figure extraction, not by moving thresholds.
  • It sees abstracts, not full texts. A finding only in the body of a paper is missed.
  • "Not enough evidence" is about this search. It means too little was found, not that none exists.
  • The thresholds are calibrated, not validated. They were set on 30 October 2026 from six runs (123 connections) and the owner's reading of examples like #4. BEAM's bootstrapped confidence interval and its human raters are not used.
  • No hierarchy of evidence types. As in BEAM, a policy evaluation or official monitoring can weigh as much as a trial, if its quality and relevance are high.
