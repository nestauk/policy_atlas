# Agentic evidence search for theory-of-change links: what the field does (research notes, 2026-10-27)

Scope: web research only, nothing in the repo changed. "Unverified" means I saw the claim only second-hand (a vendor page or a blog summary) or could not open the primary source.

The problem we have: each link got one search about the *topic* and missed the *right kind of study* (randomised trials that cut sugary drinks and measured child weight; a validated energy-balance growth model). Almost everything below is about one of four fixes: ask for the right study type, search more than once with feedback, follow citations from good hits, and stop for a stated reason.

---

## 1. Agentic and iterative literature retrieval systems

| System | What it is | Key idea we could reuse | Evidence it works | Cost and complexity |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **PaperQA2** (FutureHouse, Sept 2024; [arXiv 2409.13740](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.13740v2)); now sold as "Crow"/"Falcon" on the [FutureHouse platform](https://aibusiness.com/generative-ai/former-google-ceo-backed-startup-builds-ai-agents-for-science) (May 2025) | Agent with 4 tools: paper search (turns the question into keyword searches), gather evidence, citation traversal, answer | (a) **RCS (rerank and contextual summarise)**: an LLM reads each retrieved chunk, writes a short summary *relative to the question* and gives a 0-10 relevance score; this is the step that mattered most. (b) **Citation traversal only from strong hits**: follow citing and cited papers only for evidence scored 8 or higher, and keep papers that several strong hits share (overlap threshold of one third). | LitQA2 (248 hard questions): precision 85.2%, accuracy 66.0%, vs PhD/postdoc experts 73.8% / 67.7%. Ablations: RCS gave a large accuracy gain over plain retrieval (p<0.001); removing citation traversal barely changed accuracy but **significantly cut recall of the key papers (DOI recall, p=0.022)**. Accuracy rose from top-k 1 to 10, little gain after 10. | About $1-3 per question, ~14.5 papers parsed per question. Interesting: the agent only ran ~1.3 searches and ~0.5 citation traversals per question on average, so "many searches" is not what made it good. |
| **OpenScholar** (Ai2 + Univ. of Washington; [arXiv Nov 2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.14199v1); Nature paper 2026, [Ai2 blog](https://allenai.org/blog/openscholar-nature)) | Retrieval over 45M open-access papers (236M passage embeddings), then write, critique itself, retrieve again | **Self-feedback loop**: after a draft answer, the model writes feedback ("missing evidence on X"), which can trigger another retrieval round. A natural "search more?" decision. | On ScholarQABench (2,967 expert queries): 8B model beats GPT-4o by 5% and PaperQA2 by 7% on correctness; citation accuracy similar to human experts, while GPT-4o alone fabricated citations 78-90% of the time. | Needs its own large index; not something we would rebuild. The loop idea is cheap. |
| **PaSa** (ByteDance, ACL 2025; [arXiv 2501.10120](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.10120v2); [code](https://github.com/bytedance/pasa)) | Two agents: **Crawler** (issues searches, expands citations of a paper, or stops on that paper) and **Selector** (reads each queued paper, decides if it meets the request) | The cleanest published split of "find candidates" vs "judge candidates", with citation expansion as a tool the crawler chooses per paper. | vs Google+GPT-4o: Recall@20 +33.8%, Recall@50 +38.8%, Recall@100 +42.6% on its own test set (AutoScholarQuery, built from papers' related-work citations, mostly AI/ML). | Their best version is RL-trained (reinforcement learning); a prompted GPT-4o version also worked, just ~10% lower recall. |
| **Undermind** ([whitepaper, Jan 2024](https://www.undermind.ai/whitepaper.pdf); summary by [Aaron Tay, Apr 2024](https://aarontay.substack.com/p/undermindai-different-type-of-ai-agent?open=false)) | Commercial agent over Semantic Scholar; runs several adaptive rounds (typically about three) mixing embeddings, citation links and LLM reasoning | (a) **Next round is built from what was found**, not just the question. (b) LLM classifies into 3 bins: highly relevant / closely related / not relevant. (c) **Discovery-curve saturation estimate**: tracks how fast new relevant papers keep appearing and predicts how many are still missing. | Vendor figures: ~10x more relevant results than Google Scholar for median user query; ~2% false negatives and <4% false positives on "highly relevant". A user test found 6 of 9 included studies of a 2021 meta-analysis. **Unverified** (vendor whitepaper; I could not extract the PDF text, figures from the Tay summary). | 3-5 minutes per search. |
| **Elicit** (research agents, systematic review mode) | Semantic search plus PubMed, ClinicalTrials.gov and web; LLM screening and extraction | Searching trial registries as a separate source when the link needs trials. | Vendor ([blog, May 2026](https://elicit.com/blog/evaluating-elicit-slr)): "a single semantic search retrieves 95% of studies in the final review", tested on ~994 Cochrane reviews. **Independent study disagrees**: Lau and Golder, Cochrane Evidence Synthesis and Methods, Sept 2025 ([PMC12483133](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12483133/)): Elicit sensitivity averaged **39.5%** (25-69%) vs 94.5% for the reviews' own searches, but precision 41.8% vs 7.6%. | The gap between these two numbers is the lesson: vendor recall claims depend on how many results you count and which reviews. |
| **Consensus** ([deep dive by Aaron Tay, Nov 2025](https://aarontay.substack.com/p/a-2025-deep-dive-of-consensus-promises)) | Hybrid keyword + embedding search (~1,500 hits), rerank by relevance and "research strength", keep top 20 (or 50 in Deep Search) | LLM-extracted **study design labels** (RCT, meta-analysis, etc.) on every hit; Deep Search runs ~20 queries then citation search. | Pitfall noted: the "Consensus Meter" is vote-counting (equal weight per paper regardless of size or quality), which evidence-synthesis methods abandoned long ago. Our rule-based grade already avoids this. | We already pay per call (see memory). |
| **Ai2 Asta / Paper Finder** ([Ai2 ScholarQA blog](https://allenai.org/blog/ai2-scholarqa); [AstaBench, Oct 2025](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2510.21652)) | Decomposes the query, searches, LLM relevance judgement, forward and backward snowballing, rerank; shows each step | Showing the decomposition and each relevance judgement to the user (good for our "why was this found" audit trail). | AstaBench gives 2,400+ problems across 11 benchmarks for comparing agents; I did not extract Paper Finder's own numbers. | Free tool; open code for parts. |
| **Anthropic multi-agent research system** ([engineering blog, 13 June 2025](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/multi-agent-research-system)) | Lead agent plans, spawns sub-agents that search in parallel, then decides whether more research is needed | (a) **Effort-scaling rules written in the prompt**: simple fact = 1 agent, 3-10 tool calls; comparisons = 2-4 sub-agents with 10-15 calls each. (b) Agents default to overly specific queries; tell them to **start short and broad, then narrow**. (c) Prefer specialised tools over generic web search; tool descriptions matter a lot. (d) Start evaluating with ~20 real queries. | Multi-agent beat single-agent by 90.2% on their internal research eval. | ~4x tokens of a chat for one agent, ~15x for multi-agent. |
| **STORM** (Stanford, NAACL 2024; [arXiv 2402.14207](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2402.14207v1)) | Writes Wikipedia-style articles; generates questions from several "perspectives" before searching | **Perspective-guided question asking**: before searching, list the different angles a question can be asked from. For us the angles are evidence types (trial, model, mechanism study, policy evaluation, local data). | Better outline coverage than single-prompt generation (judged by humans and metrics). | Cheap idea; the framework itself is overkill. |
| **Deep research surveys** ([2506.18096](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2506.18096), [2508.12752](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2508.12752), both 2025) | Overviews of plan, generate sub-questions, explore, write | Confirms the common shape: plan, search loop with reflection, synthesis. | Survey papers, no single number. | n/a |

**Takeaway for section 1.** The parts with measured value are: an LLM relevance score with a short question-specific summary (PaperQA2's RCS), citation chasing seeded only from strong hits (helps recall of key papers), and a loop that plans the next search from the gaps in what was found (OpenScholar, Undermind, Anthropic). Many-agent setups cost a lot more and none of the numbers show they are needed for a question as narrow as one causal link.

## 2. Writing queries by evidence type

**PICO / PECO decomposition** (Population, Intervention or Exposure, Comparison, Outcome). This is the standard way to break a question into search concepts. Most LLM query generators use it.
- Wang, Scells, Koopman, Zuccon, "Can ChatGPT write a good Boolean query…" (SIGIR 2023, [arXiv 2302.03495](https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.03495)) and the reassessment (June 2025, [arXiv 2505.07155](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.07155v2)). Newer LLMs got average recall on CLEF TAR (the Conference and Labs of the Evaluation Forum's Technology-Assisted Review collection) from ~35% below expert queries to ~15% below. **Giving the model one or two known relevant "seed" studies roughly doubled recall (0.29 to 0.64)**. Results vary a lot between models; GPT-4 sometimes added rigid filters that returned zero relevant records. Lesson: check that a query returns something before trusting it.
- An LLM chained-prompt framework (PICO extraction, then term expansion, then Boolean query) reported 0.9 average recall on a LEADSInstruct subset ([arXiv 2602.00011](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.00011?context=cs), 2026). **Unverified**: I saw only the abstract.
- DeepRetrieval (2025, [arXiv 2503.00223](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2503.00223)): a small 3B model trained by reinforcement learning, with retrieval recall as the reward, to rewrite queries for PubMed. 65% recall on publication search vs 25% for the previous best; 71% on trial search. Shows how much query wording matters. Training our own is out of scope.

**Study-design search filters ("hedges")**. Tested, published blocks of search terms that pick out one study type. They are designed for PubMed/MEDLINE syntax, but their *vocabulary* transfers to OpenAlex keyword queries.
- Cochrane Highly Sensitive Search Strategy for randomised controlled trials (RCTs): sensitivity 0.96, precision 0.14 against 27,617 trial records (Glanville et al., J Med Libr Assoc, Oct 2020, [link](https://jmla.pitt.edu/ojs/jmla/article/view/912/1144)).
- McMaster "Clinical Queries" hedges (Haynes group) for therapy, diagnosis, prognosis, aetiology, and reviews: broad versions >90% sensitivity across MEDLINE, Embase, CINAHL and PsycINFO; narrow versions >=94% specificity but sensitivity can fall below 50% ([PubMed 21775104](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21775104/); [HIRU](https://hiruweb.mcmaster.ca/?p=1084)).
- The ISSG (InterTASC Information Specialists' Sub-Group) Search Filter Resource collects filters for other designs too (economic evaluations, observational studies, qualitative studies). **Not opened this session.**
- Reuse: keep a small table "evidence type → filter terms", e.g. RCT: `randomized OR randomised OR "controlled trial" OR placebo OR "randomly assigned"`; modelling: `"simulation model" OR "microsimulation" OR "validated model" OR "energy balance model"`; natural experiment: `"natural experiment" OR "difference-in-differences" OR "interrupted time series"`. AND it with the link's two concepts (cause, effect). This targets exactly our failure.

**Objective (data-driven) query building** (Hausner et al., IQWiG, the German Institute for Quality and Efficiency in Health Care; [Syst Rev 2012](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC3351720/); app: [searchbuildR, 2025](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11795901/)). Take a few known relevant studies, pull the most frequent distinctive words from their titles and abstracts, build the query from those, test it on held-out studies. Sensitivity 97% vs 75% for queries built from concepts alone (precision 5% vs 4%). For us: once one good study is found for a link, mine its title and abstract for the next round's terms. Same idea as the "seed study" result above.

**Mechanism vs effectiveness vs transfer questions**. Different links need different evidence:
- Effectiveness ("X reduces Y"): trials, quasi-experiments, systematic reviews.
- Mechanism ("children eat less excess energy → fewer children with obesity"): physiological or modelling studies, dose-response, validated simulation models. Clarke, Gillies, Illari, Russo and Williamson ("Mechanisms and the evidence hierarchy", Topoi 2014, [PDF](https://blogs.kent.ac.uk/jonw/files/2015/01/MechEH-published.pdf)) argue evidence of mechanism is a complement to trial evidence, not a lower rung, and is often what you need to generalise a result.
- "Holds here" (transfer): Cartwright and Hardie, *Evidence-Based Policy: A Practical Guide to Doing It Better* (2012, [LSE review](https://researchonline.lse.ac.uk/id/eprint/53946/1/Book_Review_Evidence%20Based%20Policy_A%20Practical%20Guide%20to%20Doing%20It%20Better_LSE%20Review%20of%20Books.pdf)): to claim it will work here you need (1) evidence that it worked there and (2) evidence that the *support factors* it needs are present here. Search for the support factors as their own queries (local data, policy documents, context studies). This maps directly onto our two judgements, "worked elsewhere" and "holds here".
- Behavioural or implementation links ("brands keep spend away from influencers here"): mostly grey literature, market data, regulator reports, process evaluations.

**Realist review searching** (searching for mechanisms and context). Booth and colleagues' scoping review of "the realist search" ([Exeter repository](https://ore.exeter.ac.uk/repository/handle/10871/39669)) sets out six parts: background search, search for programme theory, search for empirical studies, search to refine theory, plus documenting. Booth's **CLUSTER** method starts from one key "sibling" study and fans out via its citations, authors, related projects and grey reports to find "conceptual richness and contextual thickness". Reuse: once one good study per link is found, chase its cluster (citations, same project, same authors).

**How theory-of-change links get evidenced in practice**.
- 3ie (International Initiative for Impact Evaluation): Howard White, "Theory-based impact evaluation: principles and practice" (3ie Working Paper 3, 2009, [summary](https://gsdrc.org/document-library/theory-based-impact-evaluation-principles-and-practice/)). Map the causal chain, test each link's assumptions, and note that **many links are tested by factual analysis** (descriptive data such as who is reached, does behaviour change), not by impact studies. So not every link needs an RCT, and the "best evidence type" differs by link.
- EPPI-Centre (Evidence for Policy and Practice Information Centre, UCL): Kneale, Thomas and Harris, "Developing and optimising the use of logic models in systematic reviews" (PLOS ONE 2015, [PMC4648510](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4648510/)). Logic models can guide search strategies and inclusion criteria, but in practice were mostly just pictures and rarely used to organise findings. Our per-link checking is the less common, more useful use.
- What Works Centres (e.g. Education Endowment Foundation toolkit): grade by evidence strength across studies. **Not researched in depth this session.**

## 3. Choosing the source

- **Bibliographic databases** (OpenAlex, PubMed, Semantic Scholar) are best for trials, cohort studies, models and reviews. Trial registries (ClinicalTrials.gov, ISRCTN) help when the link needs trials (Elicit searches them).
- **Grey literature / policy repositories** (Overton) are needed for implementation, regulation and context links. Adams et al. (2016, [Durham repository](https://dro.dur.ac.uk/19968)): in public health, evidence on the range of interventions and many evaluations "may be predominantly, or only" in grey literature. Overton indexes ~19M policy documents, but only 7.7% contain scholarly references, and coverage is skewed by country, language and topic ([arXiv 2601.01764](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2601.01764), 2026). So treat a miss in Overton as "not found here", not as "does not exist".
- **Web** is for very recent, local or market facts (ad spend, platform policies). The Anthropic post advises specialised tools before generic web search.
- **LLM tool routing**. Federated search research ("resource selection": which engine to send a query to) shows LLMs can pick sources zero-shot: ReSLLM (2024, [review](https://themoonlight.io/review/resllm-large-language-models-are-strong-resource-selectors-for-federated-search)); RAGRoute (2025, [arXiv 2502.19280](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2502.19280)), a light classifier router. Main point: send queries to **several sources** rather than forcing one choice. Aaron Tay's "invisible menu problem" ([Apr 2026](https://aarontay.substack.com/p/from-fixed-search-workflows-to-agentic)) warns that agents (and users) don't use tools they aren't clearly told about, so the planner prompt should list each source with what it is good for.
- Practical rule for us: route by **evidence type**, not by topic. Trial, model or review → OpenAlex (with design filter terms); regulation, implementation or local context → Overton then web; "holds here" support factors → Overton plus web plus local statistics.

## 4. When to stop, and cost control

- **Statistical stopping for screening** (Callaghan and Müller-Hansen, Systematic Reviews 2020, [PMC7700715](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7700715/)): stop when you can reject "we have missed more than X% of relevant items" at a chosen confidence, using a hypergeometric test on a random sample. Reliable recall with about 17% average work saved. Designed for ranked screening of a fixed pool, not for open-ended search, but the attitude (say what recall you are claiming and how sure you are) carries over.
- **Knee, target and budget rules** for technology-assisted review ([Cormack and Grossman-style heuristics, arXiv 2106.09871](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2106.09871)): "knee" stops when the cumulative-relevant curve flattens (diminishing returns); "target" samples randomly until k=10 relevant are found, then stops when all are found again (aims at ≥70% recall); "budget" is a fixed review allowance. Newer: Chao's species-richness estimator ([arXiv 2404.01176](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2404.01176), 2024), which estimates the total from how often items are re-found. That is close to Undermind's discovery curve.
- **Re-find (capture-recapture) signal for agents**: when several different queries keep returning the same relevant papers and new rounds add nothing new, the search is saturated. Cheap to compute for us: "share of relevant hits in this round that were already seen".
- **"Enough evidence" rules**: Google's "Sufficient Context" (ICLR 2025, [blog](https://research.google/blog/deeper-insights-into-retrieval-augmented-generation-the-role-of-sufficient-context/); arXiv ID 2411.06037 from memory, not checked): ask an LLM whether the retrieved material is enough to answer, rather than whether it is relevant; insufficient context raises error rates a lot. For us: stop when the judge says the link has a decisive study of the right type (e.g. ≥1 strong-grade "supports" or "challenges"), otherwise search again with a new evidence type.
- **Cost control seen in practice**: hard caps on rounds (Undermind ~3, PaperQA2 averages ~1.3 searches), effort scaled by question (Anthropic's rules), cheap model for screening plus expensive model for the final judgement, LLM summary of each chunk to cut tokens (RCS kept chunks to 200-400 tokens with no accuracy loss).

## 5. Evaluating this at small scale

- **Gold sets from systematic reviews' included studies**: the standard method (Elicit, Lau and Golder, Hausner, CLEF TAR all use it). CLEF TAR 2017-2019 and eight more collections are bundled in **CSMeD** (NeurIPS 2023 Datasets track, [arXiv 2311.12474](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2311.12474)): 325 reviews (medicine and computer science) with included studies. Caveat (Lau and Golder vs Elicit): recall depends heavily on how many results you count, so report recall@k at fixed k (e.g. 20, our cap).
- **Recall of known key studies**: our failure is exactly this. Pick 10-20 links, write down by hand the 1-3 studies that *should* decide each one (from the review that answers it), and measure "found at all" and "found within the 20-record cap". PaperQA2's DOI recall and Anthropic's ~20-query starter eval are this size.
- **Claim-verification benchmarks with retrieval**: SciFact-Open (EMNLP Findings 2022, [arXiv 2210.13777](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2210.13777)): 500K abstracts; systems trained on small corpora dropped at least 15 F1 points. Labels come from pooling top results of 4 systems, so evidence none of them found is unlabelled (recall is overstated). HealthVer (EMNLP Findings 2021, [ACL](https://aclweb.org/anthology/2021.findings-emnlp.297.pdf)) and Check-COVID (ACL Findings 2023, [arXiv 2305.18265](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2305.18265), 1,504 news claims) are COVID-only. Useful to sanity-check the judge's supports/challenges/not-about labels, not for testing our retrieval.
- **Retrieval benchmarks for literature search**: LitSearch (EMNLP 2024, [arXiv 2407.18940](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.18940v2)): 597 ML/NLP queries; best dense retriever recall@5 74.8%, BM25 (keyword ranking) 24.8 points lower, GPT-4o reranking +4.4. Wrong field for us, but shows embedding search plus LLM rerank beats keyword search alone.
- **LLM-as-judge pitfalls**: Clarke and Dietz, "LLM-based relevance assessment still can't replace human relevance assessment" (Dec 2024, [arXiv 2412.17156](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.17156)). High overall agreement hides disagreement on the *top* systems; they built a system that games the LLM judge to get inflated scores. Practical consequences for us: (1) do not use the same LLM prompt to both pick results and grade the retrieval; (2) hand-label a small sample each time the prompt changes; (3) measure retrieval with fixed known-study lists, not only with the judge. LLM screening meta-analysis (JMIR 2024, [e52758](https://www.jmir.org/2024//e52758)): pooled sensitivity 97.1% but specificity only 37.7%, with large spread between models and prompts. LLM screens tend to be over-inclusive, which is fine as long as a later judge does the filtering.

## What we should copy (ranked by expected value for Policy Atlas)

1. **Plan per link by evidence type before searching.** For each link the LLM names 1-3 target evidence types (effectiveness: RCT, quasi-experiment or review; mechanism: dose-response or validated model; holds-here: support factors, local data or policy documents), following Cartwright and Hardie's "worked there" vs "support factors here" split and White's point that some links need descriptive facts rather than impact studies. Then write one small query per type. This fixes the failure we saw directly. Cost: one extra LLM call per link.
2. **A fixed table of design filter terms** (adapted from the Cochrane RCT filter, McMaster hedges and the ISSG filters) ANDed with the link's cause and effect concepts, with each query checked for "returns at least one hit". Cheap, well tested (0.96 sensitivity for trials in MEDLINE; transfer to OpenAlex keyword search is untested).
3. **Seed-and-chase after the first good hit.** Once a hit is judged relevant and of the right type, (a) follow its citations both ways (PaperQA2: only from strong hits; this significantly raised key-paper recall), and (b) mine its title and abstract for the next query's terms (Hausner's objective approach and the seed-study result: recall 0.29 to 0.64). OpenAlex gives `referenced_works` and `cites:` filters for free.
4. **Small loop with an explicit stopping rule.** At most 3 rounds per link. Stop early when (a) a decisive study of the target type is judged supports or challenges, or (b) a round adds no new relevant records (saturation / knee). Otherwise the planner names which evidence type is still missing and searches for that. Record the stop reason on the link.
5. **A known-key-studies eval before building more.** 10-20 links with hand-picked decisive studies, recall@20 per link, run old vs new pipeline. A small dataset the team can own; avoids relying on the LLM judge to score itself.
6. **Route by evidence type, query several sources.** Trials, models and reviews → OpenAlex; implementation, regulation and local context → Overton then web. List each tool and what it is for in the planner prompt (the "invisible menu" lesson). Treat "nothing in Overton" as unknown, not as absence.
7. **Relevance step that scores and summarises against the link** (PaperQA2's RCS) instead of a yes/no screen: gives the loop a score to rank on and a reason to show the user. Probably a small change to the existing screen.
8. (Lower value now) Multi-agent fan-out, RL-trained query writers (DeepRetrieval, PaSa) and discovery-curve estimators: real gains reported, but 4-15x token cost or training data we don't have. Revisit only if items 1-5 still miss key studies.
