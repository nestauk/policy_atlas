# Implementation aspects for the longlist profile

Research note for task 046. Date: 2026-09-29. Status: input for the owner, not a decision.

**How to read this note.** Sections 1 to 3 and 6 report what the sources say. Sections 4 and 5 are recommendations. Each source has a tag in square brackets, for example [S3]. The reference list at the end gives the full reference, the URL, and how far the source was checked. "Opened" means the text was read for this note. "Not verified" means it was not opened, and the claim rests on a secondary page or on general knowledge.

## Summary

- The sources agree on a core: cost, time, workforce and skill, legal basis, dependencies, and complexity. The owner's draft already covers this core.
- The owner's doubt is right. No source defines complexity as only the number of bodies. The sources define it by components, behaviours, skill, levels, and tailoring.
- Recommendation: split the draft "complexity" into **coordination** (how many bodies) and **delivery complexity** (how intricate the action is for each case). Add **acceptability** as a cautious eighth aspect.
- Every band is a guess at this stage. The literature says early guesses lean optimistic. The profile should say so, and should allow "cannot judge".

## 1. What implementation science says

| Framework | What it names | Useful for the profile |
|---|---|---|
| CFIR 2009 [S1] and CFIR 2022 [S2] | Five domains: innovation, outer setting, inner setting, individuals, implementation process | Innovation constructs: complexity, cost, adaptability, trialability. Setting constructs: policies and laws, financing, partnerships, available resources, compatibility |
| Proctor et al. implementation outcomes [S3] | Acceptability, adoption, appropriateness, feasibility, fidelity, implementation cost, penetration, sustainability | Says which outcomes matter early and which matter late |
| RE-AIM [S4] | Reach, effectiveness, adoption, implementation, maintenance | "Implementation" includes time and cost |
| MRC guidance 2008 [S5] and 2021 [S6] | What makes an intervention complex | See section 3 |
| iCAT_SR [S7] | Ten dimensions of intervention complexity | See section 3 |
| TIDieR [S8] | Twelve items to describe an intervention | A checklist of what a design must state before anyone can judge delivery |
| Normalisation process theory [S9] | Four kinds of work: coherence, cognitive participation, collective action, reflexive monitoring | Implementation is work that people must do. Limited use at longlist stage |
| NASSS [S10] | Seven domains, each rated simple, complicated or complex | See section 3 |

**CFIR 2022** defines its constructs as short statements [S2]. The ones that matter here:

- Innovation complexity: "The innovation is complicated, which may be reflected by its scope and/or the nature and number of connections and steps".
- Innovation cost: "The innovation purchase and operating costs are affordable".
- Innovation trialability: "The innovation can be tested or piloted on a small scale and undone".
- Policies and laws: "Legislation, regulations, professional group guidelines and recommendations, or accreditation standards support implementation and/or delivery of the innovation".
- Available resources: "Resources are available to implement and deliver the innovation".
- Compatibility: "The innovation fits with workflows, systems, and processes".

**Proctor et al.** define feasibility as "the extent to which a new treatment, or an innovation, can be successfully used or carried out within a given agency or setting" [S3]. They define acceptability as "the perception among implementation stakeholders that a given treatment, service, practice, or innovation is agreeable, palatable, or satisfactory" [S3]. They define implementation cost as "the cost impact of an implementation effort", and they say it varies with the complexity of the intervention, the implementation strategy, and the setting [S3]. In their table, acceptability, appropriateness, feasibility and cost matter early. Penetration and sustainability matter late [S3].

**RE-AIM** defines implementation as "the intervention agents' fidelity to the various elements of an intervention's protocol, including consistency of delivery as intended and the time and cost of the intervention" [S4]. It defines reach as "the absolute number, proportion, and representativeness of individuals who are willing to participate" [S4].

**TIDieR** lists twelve items: brief name, why, what (materials), what (procedures), who provided, how, where, when and how much, tailoring, modifications, how well (planned), how well (actual) [S8]. "Who provided", "where", "when and how much" and "tailoring" map directly to workforce, dependencies, time, and complexity.

**Normalisation process theory** says that practices become routine "as the result of people working, individually and collectively, to enact them" [S9]. It is a theory of process. It does not give a property of an option that a reader can band from a design. It supports one point: the effort asked of front-line staff is a real cost.

## 2. What government appraisal and policy analysis use

**HM Treasury Green Book (2026 edition)** [S11]. The Five Case Model has five cases: strategic, economic, commercial, financial, management (para 3.12). Longlist appraisal tests options against objectives and critical success factors (para 5.9). Table 4 gives five factors:

| Critical success factor | Green Book description (verbatim, Table 4) |
|---|---|
| Objectives and strategic fit | "The proposal meets its SMART objectives." "The proposal has strategic fit with other policies, portfolios, programmes and projects being undertaken in the public sector." |
| Value for money | "The proposal represents the optimal use of public resources to achieve its objectives." |
| Supplier capacity and capability | "Potential suppliers can feasibly provide the inputs that are required for the proposal." |
| Affordability | "The proposal is affordable for all of the public bodies involved." |
| Achievability | "There are robust arrangements in place to deliver, monitor and evaluate the proposal." |

Three more points from the Green Book matter here.

- Constraints: "Examples of constraints might include legal requirements, ethical standards, social acceptability and timing" (para 5.6).
- Dependencies: "external factors on which an option is reliant to be successful but which are beyond its direct control" (para 5.11).
- Precision: longlist appraisal "typically uses indicative estimates and ranges, which should support an informed judgement about what options are viable for shortlisting" (para 5.10).

**UK delivery assurance.** The Infrastructure and Projects Authority defined a delivery confidence assessment as "an assessment of the likelihood of a project delivering its objectives to time and cost" [S12]. It rates a defined project with a plan and a budget. It does not rate a kind of action. It is a model for a coarse band with a reason, and nothing more at this stage.

**OECD.** Viennet and Pont name the determinants of effective implementation, among them "smart policy design" and "inclusive stakeholder engagement" [S13]. On feasibility they warn that law makers may "focus more on what is doable politically than practically", and that "the resources or technology to make the policy work may not be readily available" [S13, section 3.1.3]. The paper covers education policy, but the framework is general.

**Policy capacity.** Wu, Ramesh and Howlett define policy capacity as "the set of skills and resources—or competences and capabilities—necessary to perform policy functions" [S14]. They name three competences (analytical, operational, political) at three levels (individual, organisational, systemic). Operational capacity "allows the alignment of resources with policy actions so that they can be implemented in practice". Political capacity "helps to obtain and sustain political support" [S14].

**State capability.** Pritchett, Woolcock and Andrews describe tasks that are "transaction intensive", and tasks that need "high discretionary decision-making and numerous face-to-face transactions" [S15]. They name health and education as examples. They also name "premature load bearing": asking a system to do more than it can [S15]. This is a second, independent source for the owner's point: a task that needs many case-by-case judgements by skilled staff is harder to deliver than a rule applied by a system.

**Policy analysis textbooks.** Secondary sources report that Bardach lists legality, political acceptability, robustness under implementation, and improvability as practical criteria [S16, book not verified]. Weimer and Vining treat political feasibility as a matter of actors, their motives, beliefs and resources [S17, book not verified].

**Policy instruments.** Hood sorts the tools of government by the resource they use: nodality (information), authority, treasure (money), organisation (direct action) [S18, book not verified]. Salamon rates tools on coerciveness, directness, automaticity and visibility, and judges them on five criteria including manageability and legitimacy [S19, book not verified]. Automaticity is the extent to which a tool uses an existing administrative structure [S19, as reported by a secondary source]. The Nuffield Council on Bioethics "intervention ladder" orders public health measures from "do nothing" to "eliminate choice" by how much they intrude on choice [S20, report not verified]. These sources suggest that the kind of instrument predicts part of the burden. Information and defaults ask little of the state. Direct services ask a lot. Coercive tools ask for legal powers and meet more resistance.

## 3. What "complexity" means

| Component of complexity | MRC 2008 [S5] | MRC 2021 [S6] | iCAT_SR [S7] | CFIR [S1][S2] | NASSS [S10] |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Number of (interacting) components | yes | yes | yes | "connections and steps" | "few components" = simple |
| Number and difficulty of behaviours asked of those who deliver or receive | yes | "range of behaviours targeted" | yes | | |
| Skill needed by those who deliver | | yes | yes | | |
| Skill needed by those who receive | | yes | yes | | |
| Number of groups, settings or organisational levels | yes | yes | yes | "scope" | organisation domain |
| Degree of tailoring or flexibility | yes | yes | yes | | |
| Dependence on context | | yes | yes (optional) | | wider context domain |
| Duration, radicalness, disruptiveness | | | | yes (2009) | |
| Unpredictability | | | | | "complex" |

The key definitions, verbatim:

- MRC 2008, Box 1: "Number of interacting components"; "Number and difficulty of behaviours required by those delivering or receiving the intervention"; "Number of groups or organisational levels targeted by the intervention"; "Number and variability of outcomes"; "Degree of flexibility or tailoring of the intervention permitted" [S5].
- MRC 2021: "An intervention might be considered complex because of properties of the intervention itself, such as the number of components involved; the range of behaviours targeted; expertise and skills required by those delivering and receiving the intervention; the number of groups, settings, or levels targeted; or the permitted level of flexibility of the intervention or its components" [S6]. It adds that complexity can also come from the interaction between the intervention and its context [S6].
- CFIR 2009: "Perceived difficulty of implementation, reflected by duration, scope, radicalness, disruptiveness, centrality, and intricacy and number of steps required to implement" [S1].
- NASSS: simple is "straightforward, predictable, few components". Complicated is "multiple interacting components or issues". Complex is "dynamic, unpredictable, not easily disaggregated into constituent components" [S10]. Programmes with complexity in many domains "rarely, if ever, became mainstreamed" [S10].
- Rogers: complexity is the degree to which an innovation is perceived as relatively difficult to understand and use [S21, book not verified].

**The owner's example.** A nudge has one component. It asks one small behaviour of the receiver. It needs no trained deliverer and no judgement for each case. It is the same for everyone. It is low on almost every row of the table. A clinical intervention has many components. Trained staff deliver it. They tailor it to each person. It asks hard, repeated behaviours of the receiver. It is high on almost every row. The number of bodies is one row out of nine. So the owner's doubt matches the literature.

One caution: a nudge can still be hard to deliver for other reasons. A default in the tax system may depend on an IT change. That is a dependency, not complexity. The aspects must stay separate for this reason.

## 4. Recommended aspects

This section is a recommendation, not a finding.

Bands should be relative: low, medium or high compared with typical public programmes in the same domain. Each aspect also needs a fourth value: "cannot judge".

Notes on the set:

- Skill sits in workforce, not in delivery complexity. The MRC counts skill as part of complexity [S6]. The split avoids counting it twice.
- If the owner wants fewer aspects, merge 6 and 7 into one "complexity" band, and make the reason line name the driver. This loses information: a cross-government nudge and a single-agency clinical service would get the same band.
- Acceptability is the least safe aspect. It is included because the Green Book names social acceptability as a constraint at longlist stage [S11], and Proctor et al. place acceptability early [S3].

## 6. Warnings

**What the sources say.**

1. Early estimates lean optimistic. The Green Book defines optimism bias as "the demonstrated systematic tendency for practitioners to be over-optimistic about key assumptions in appraisal, such as social costs, social benefits or project duration" [S11, para 6.78]. The supplementary guidance adds that "higher optimism bias adjustments may therefore be required at an earlier stage in the appraisal process" [S22, para 3.3]. Its upper bound for capital cost on equipment and development projects is 200 per cent [S22, Table 1].
2. The inside view causes the error. Kahneman and Lovallo say that forecasters treat each problem as unique and build a scenario from the plan. They call this the inside view. The outside view uses the results of similar past cases [S23, as reported by the publisher's abstract and secondary pages]. Buehler, Griffin and Ross found that people underestimate their own completion times because they focus on the plan and not on past experience [S24, not opened].
3. Reference class forecasting is the remedy. Flyvbjerg says it "achieves accuracy by basing forecasts on actual performance in a reference class of comparable projects and thereby bypassing both optimism bias and strategic misrepresentation" [S25].
4. Feasibility belongs to a setting. Proctor et al. define it "within a given agency or setting" [S3]. MRC 2021 says complexity also comes from context [S6]. A band for a kind of action is a statement about a typical setting only.
5. Do not add the bands into a score. The Green Book "recommends against the use of multi-criteria analysis (MCA) in decision making", because simple weighting and scoring "lacks an objective basis and can reduce transparency" [S11, para 5.15].
6. Published intervention descriptions are often incomplete. TIDieR exists because of this [S8]. Abstracts are shorter still.

**What this note recommends.**

- Ask the model to reason from the class: "options of this kind have usually taken...". Do not let it build a best-case plan.
- When the model is unsure between two bands, it should choose the harder one and say why.
- Show the bands as a profile. Do not sum them or rank by them.
- State on the card that the profile is a judgement made before assessment, and that it assumes a typical setting.
- Abstracts mostly report trials that ran under good conditions. They under-report cost, staffing and failed delivery. The model's general knowledge will carry most of the profile, so the reason line should say when an abstract supports it and when it does not.
