## 1. Official appraisal guidance

### 1.1 HM Treasury Green Book

There are two editions to know. The 2022 edition [2] was in force until February 2026. The 2026 edition [1] replaced it after the Green Book Review 2025. The method is the same in both. The 2026 text is shorter and less rigid.

**What a longlist is for.** "A longlist is a wide range of potential options for achieving the objectives of the proposal." It helps practitioners "to think broadly and creatively" and "reduces the risk of overlooking better options" [1, para 5.1]. The 2026 edition says these steps "are often overlooked in appraisals" and that practitioners "are often tempted to skip these stages" [1, para 5.3].

**The sequence.** Both editions use the same order: objectives and business as usual; critical success factors; longlist; shortlist with a preferred way forward; detailed appraisal of the shortlist [1, ch. 2 and 5; 2, ch. 4].

**SMART objectives.** The 2022 edition says "Up to 5 or 6 SMART objectives should be established" [2, para 4.11]. The 2026 edition says "A proposal should ideally have as few objectives as possible" [1, para 4.23].

**Business as usual.** It is "the outcome that is expected if current arrangements continue and the proposal under consideration is not implemented" [1, para 4.14]. "BAU does not mean doing nothing" [1, para 4.15]. It always goes to the shortlist, "regardless of whether or not it meets the objectives and critical success factors" [1, para 5.18].

**Critical success factors (CSFs).** They are "the attributes that an option must have in order to achieve the proposal's objectives successfully" [1, para 5.5]. There are five standard ones. They mirror the Five Case Model [1, Table 4; 2, Box 9]:

- objectives and strategic fit;
- value for money: "the optimal use of public resources to achieve its objectives";
- supplier capacity and capability;
- affordability: "affordable for all of the public bodies involved";
- achievability.

**Constraints.** The 2022 edition defines them as "external considerations that set limits, within which a proposal must work, for example the law, ethics, social acceptability, timing, practicality and strategic fit with wider public policies and strategy" [2, para 4.12]. The 2026 edition folds them into the CSFs: "A proposal's critical success factors might also include constraints. These are external considerations that set limits on the viability of different options" [1, para 5.6].

**Dependencies.** They are "external factors on which an option is reliant to be successful but which are beyond its direct control" [1, para 5.11].

**The options framework filter.** Options are broken into choices under five categories, taken in order [1, para 5.25]:

| Category | Question |
|---|---|
| Scope | "What is the coverage of the service to be delivered?" |
| Solution | "How can the outcomes be delivered?" Examples include "regulations, taxes, grants, subsidies or public information campaigns". |
| Delivery | "Which organisation will deliver the scope and solution?" |
| Implementation | Pilot, phased rollout or rapid implementation. |
| Funding | "requires an initial indicative cost estimate" |

Each choice gets a rating. Red means it "does not meet the critical success factors" and is discarded. Green is the choice that "best meets" them. Amber meets them, "but not as well" [1, paras 5.21 and 5.26]. Options are then built from green and amber choices.

The 2022 edition called the filter "required best practice" [2, para 4.24]. The 2026 edition says it is recommended but "not mandatory" [1, para 5.13]. It names SWOT analysis and swing-weighted MCDA as other routes [1, paras 5.14 and 5.16].

**The rule for exclusion.** "Any option that does not meet the objectives or the critical success factors should not proceed to shortlist appraisal" [1, para 5.9]. Reasons must be recorded: practitioners "should explain transparently what options have been considered as well as the reasons they have or have not been progressed" [1, para 5.4].

**The shortlist.** "A shortlist should generally include around five options." This means "decision makers are presented with genuine choices rather than a single pre-determined outcome" [1, para 5.17]. The five are typically [1, para 5.18]:

- business as usual;
- a do minimum, which "just achieves the proposal's objectives";
- a preferred way forward;
- a more ambitious version of it;
- a less ambitious version of it.

Other options may be added. They "may involve different outputs or different design choices" [1, para 5.19].

**Preferred way forward.** It is the front runner at the end of the longlist stage. It is not the preferred option. "Shortlist appraisal may reveal... that the preferred option is actually one of the other shortlisted options" [1, para 5.20].

### 1.2 HM Treasury business case guidance

The Project Business Case guide [3] says the case "should identify a minimum of four shortlisted options". It defines constraints as "the external conditions and agreed parameters within which the project must be delivered, over which the project has little or no control". It lists five tests for the cut from long list to short list:

1. Does the option fail to deliver the objectives and CSFs?
2. Does it "appear unlikely to deliver sufficient benefits"?
3. Is it "clearly impractical or unfeasible"?
4. Is it "clearly inferior to another, because it has greater costs and lower benefits"?
5. Does it "violate any of the constraints – for example, are any clearly unaffordable"?

Note the word "clearly" in three of the five.

### 1.3 Magenta Book

The Magenta Book [4] is guidance on evaluation. It does not set a longlisting method. It matters here for one idea. It separates internal validity from external validity, "whether the estimate is a valid representation of what might happen... when the intervention is scaled up or rolled out" [4, footnote 30]. That is our transferability question.

### 1.4 European Commission: Better Regulation toolbox, Tool #16

Tool #16 [5, pp. 115–122] has four steps: build the baseline; compile a wide range of options; "Identify the most viable options; explain the discarded policy options"; describe the retained options in detail.

It gives nine screening criteria [5, pp. 118–119]: legal feasibility, technical feasibility, previous policy choices, coherence with other policy objectives, effectiveness and efficiency, proportionality, political feasibility, relevance, and identifiability.

Three points stand out:

- "Excluding options at this stage should be clearly justified. Reasons should be as clear, self-evident and indisputable as possible." Discarded options go in "a separate section".
- On effectiveness: "It may already be possible to show that some options would with certainty achieve a worse cost-benefit balance than some alternatives." The test is certainty.
- On political feasibility: exclusion "does not mean that such options should not be mentioned or not be subject to at least a minimal assessment".

It also separates a policy **measure** from a policy **option**. "A policy option is a combination (or a package) of policy measures" [5, p. 120].

### 1.5 Australia: Office of Impact Analysis

The guide [6, Question 3] asks for "a range of genuine and viable alternative policy options". The number of options should fit the size of the problem, "but three is the minimum requirement". That count includes the status quo. One option must be non-regulatory. Options "should not be discounted just because they haven't been considered before or there are risks associated with them". And "presenting one option as a fait accompli is not acceptable".

### 1.6 New Zealand Treasury: Better Business Cases

The March 2026 guidance [7] is the clearest on pass or fail. "A critical success factor is a minimum condition that must be met... Critical success factors are applied as pass/fail tests." "The role of critical success factors is not to compare or rank options" [7, s. 6.12]. A choice is viable if it aligns with an objective and "does not fail any critical success factor".

On the shortlist [7, s. 7.9]: "Most business cases should include 3–6 shortlist options plus the baseline." Each must be distinct. "Options that differ only in minor detail, scale, or implementation refinement... should be combined or excluded." A good shortlist "avoids clustering around a single preferred direction".

### 1.7 OECD

The OECD principles [8] say: "All plausible alternatives, including non-regulatory solutions must be taken into account. A 'do-nothing option'... should always be included." Reviewers should check whether the analysis "only presents the preferred regulatory approach (perhaps with some unrealistic straw men alternatives)".

## 2. Policy analysis textbooks and MCDA

### 2.1 Bardach (secondary)

Bardach's eight steps include: construct the alternatives, select the criteria, project the outcomes, confront the trade-offs. I did not open the book. Verbatim quotes below come from a teaching site that cites the fourth edition with page numbers [9].

- Alternatives are "policy options," or "alternative courses of action," or "alternative strategies of intervention to solve or mitigate the problem" [9, citing p. 16].
- On projecting outcomes: "This is the hardest step in the Eightfold Path" [9, citing p. 47].
- Secondary summaries [10] report three further points. Start with a broad list and end with a few. Include "let present trends continue" as an alternative. Apply criteria to projected outcomes, not to the alternatives themselves.
- Bardach splits **evaluative criteria** (efficiency, equity and others) from **practical criteria** (legality, political acceptability, robustness, improvability). Secondary sources [10, 11] report this list. Not verified against the book.

### 2.2 Weimer and Vining (secondary)

A summary by Cairney [12] cites the sixth edition. Analysts should "compare specific and well-worked alternatives", including current policy. On prediction it quotes: "your predictions of the impacts of a unique policy alternative must necessarily be guided by logic and theory, rather than systematic empirical evidence" [12, citing p. 27]. My recollection is that the book treats a constraint as a goal that must be met. Not verified.

### 2.3 Dunn (secondary)

Cairney's summary [13] says Dunn's prescription methods give "a consistent way to compare each potential solution, in terms of its feasibility and predicted outcome". My recollection is that Dunn lists six constraint types (physical, legal, organisational, political, distributional, budgetary). I could not confirm this. Not verified.

### 2.4 The DCLG multi-criteria analysis manual

The manual [14] gives two screening tools that need no weights.

- **Dominance.** "Dominance occurs when one option performs at least as well as another on all criteria and strictly better than the other on at least one criterion" [14, s. 4.5]. It is rare in practice.
- **Thresholds.** "The conjunctive model eliminates options that fail to reach externally set levels of performance on each of one or more named criteria" [14, Appendix 2, p. 116].

It adds a caution. Threshold screening is valid if benchmarks "are set independently and represent accepted reasons (e.g., a safety threshold)". If thresholds are set "in the light of knowledge of what all the options are scoring... the process may induce unforeseen bias" [14, p. 116].

The Green Book now goes further. It "recommends against the use of multi-criteria analysis (MCA) in decision making", because simple weighting and scoring "lacks an objective basis and can reduce transparency" [1, para 5.15].

## 3. Constraints and criteria: how the sources tell them apart

### 3.1 The test the sources use

The sources agree on a two-part test.

- A **constraint** is external. The adopter does not control it. It sets a limit [2, para 4.12; 3]. It is pass or fail [7, s. 6.12].
- A **criterion** measures how well an option performs. It ranks or informs. Trade-offs between criteria are allowed [14, s. 4.5].

They also agree on a standard of proof for exclusion. The option must *clearly* fail [3]. The reason must be "self-evident and indisputable" [5]. The threshold must be set in advance [14].

One complication. The Green Book CSFs are used both ways. Red is a hard fail. Green against amber is a ranking [1, para 5.26]. New Zealand forbids the ranking use [7].

### 3.2 Mapping to our five kinds

| Our kind | Terms in the sources | Hard or soft in the sources |
|---|---|---|
| **Boundary** (what the option is) | Green Book "scope" and "solution" choices [1]; constraints of ethics and strategic fit [2]; EC "previous policy choices", "coherence" [5] | Hard. But the EC asks for "compelling evidence" before revisiting a past choice, so it is a stated ruling, not a judgement. |
| **Authority** (who has the power) | EC "legal feasibility" and the principle of conferral [5]; Green Book "the law" [2]; Bardach "legality" [10] | Hard. |
| **Implementation consideration** (what the adopter has or lacks) | CSFs: supplier capacity, affordability, achievability [1]; dependencies [1]; EC "technical feasibility" [5]; "clearly unaffordable" [3] | Mixed. Hard only when the failure is clear. Otherwise amber. |
| **Transferability consideration** | Magenta Book "external validity" [4]; EMMIE "moderators" [17]; EEF "Behind the Average" [15] | Soft. No source excludes on it. Appraisal guidance barely names it at the screening stage. |
| **Aim** (the outcome wanted) | SMART objectives [1]; EC "relevance" [5]; OIA "can achieve your stated policy objectives" [6] | Hard. An option that cannot serve the objective is out. |

### 3.3 Where our split is missing a kind

1. **Acceptability.** Ethics, social acceptability and political feasibility appear in the Green Book [2, para 4.12], the EC toolbox [5] and Bardach [10]. We have no place for "ministers will not do this" or "the public will not accept this".
2. **Timing.** "Timing" is a named constraint [2, para 4.12; 3]. A deadline is not a resource the adopter lacks. It is a limit on the decision.
3. **Dependencies.** An option may rely on something outside the adopter's control [1, para 5.11]. This is neither authority nor resources.
4. **Sameness and dominance.** The EC "identifiability" test [5], the NZ distinctness test [7] and dominance [14] remove options for reasons that are not constraints at all. They belong to the cut, not to the constraint step.

### 3.4 Where our split mixes two things

1. **Authority mixes competence with lawfulness.** "Who has the power to adopt it" is one question. "Is it lawful for anyone" (rights, treaties, ethics) is another. The EC treats both under legal feasibility but they behave differently. An option outside the user's power can still be adopted by another body. An unlawful option cannot.
2. **Implementation mixes a ceiling with a capability.** A stated budget cap is a limit. Staff skill is a matter of degree. The guidance treats affordability, supplier capacity and achievability as three separate CSFs [1].
3. **Aim mixes a filter with a criterion.** "Does it address the aim at all" is a filter (relevance). "How much does it move the outcome" is a criterion (effectiveness). The sources keep these apart [5].

## 4. How much evidence at each stage

### 4.1 What the sources say

Rough estimates at the longlist stage are accepted practice. The sources say so in plain terms.

- The longlist stage "includes the estimation of indicative social costs and benefits... These indicative values should be expressed as ranges" [2, para 4.3].
- The estimates are "not accurate enough to define the final option" but "good enough to support selection of a viable shortlist" [2, para 4.26].
- Longlist appraisal "does not necessarily require extensive research and analysis of costs, benefits and risks just yet. That takes place at the shortlist appraisal stage." It "typically uses indicative estimates and ranges, which should support an informed judgement about what options are viable" [1, para 5.10].
- In New Zealand, CSFs at this stage "guide judgment – not detailed scoring or quantification" [7, s. 6.12].

### 4.2 The cautions

| Caution | Source |
|---|---|
| Use ranges, not points. | [2, para 4.3] |
| Cite the evidence and the assumptions behind each judgement. | [2, para 4.34; 1, para 5.23] |
| Exclude on merit only when the case is clear or certain. | [3; 5] |
| Adjust for optimism bias. | [1, para 2.25] |
| Beware "a single and often spuriously accurate" figure. | [20, para 2.4] |

## 5. Comparable evidence-synthesis practice

### 5.1 How the toolkits present many options

| Tool | Grain of an "option" | Shown per option | Source of the numbers |
|---|---|---|---|
| EEF Teaching and Learning Toolkit [15] | Approach type ("over 40 approaches", for example feedback) | Impact in months, cost on 5 bands, evidence strength (padlocks) | Systematic review and meta-analysis of studies that meet set criteria |
| YEF Toolkit [16] | Approach type | Impact rating, evidence quality (1 to 5), cost on 3 bands | Meta-analysis of primary studies drawn from an evidence gap map |
| Crime Reduction Toolkit, EMMIE [17, 18] | Intervention type | Effect, Mechanism, Moderators, Implementation, Economic cost, each with a quality score | Systematic reviews: the toolkit "took as its unit of analysis systematic reviews" [17] |
| Foundations Guidebook (was EIF) [19] | Named programme: "clearly defined and replicable" with "a manual or other documentation" | Evidence rating, cost rating, outcomes, target group | Assessment of individual evaluations |
| 3ie evidence gap map [21, 22] | Cell in a grid of interventions and outcomes | Count and type of studies. No effect. | Coded study characteristics |

Four design lessons follow from these documents.

1. **Effect and evidence strength are separate fields.** None of the toolkits merges them. EEF warns that a large effect with weak evidence "may be less reliable" [15, p. 5].
2. **Effect is withheld when evidence is thin.** With fewer than 10 studies, EEF gives zero padlocks "and months' progress are not communicated" [15, p. 7].
3. **Cost is a band, not a figure.** EEF uses five bands. YEF uses three [15, 16].
4. **An average hides variation.** EEF has a "Behind the Average" section [15]. YEF says the rating "refers to the average impact" [16]. EMMIE gives Moderators their own column [17].

The toolkits also refuse to promise. EEF: "The Toolkits do not make definitive claims as to what will work to improve outcomes in a given school" [15, p. 4].

### 5.2 Maps do not report effects

An evidence gap map stops short of findings. Evidence maps "limit data extraction and analysis to study characteristics, without any formal synthesis of the studies' findings" [21, p. 3]. 3ie maps "do not provide any formal synthesis or explicit recommendations" [21]. They rate confidence for systematic reviews only, because appraising each primary study "takes around 2–4 hours per study" [21, footnote 3].

A longlist built from abstracts is closer to a map than to a toolkit.

### 5.3 Abstracts and secondary sources

In the guidance I read, abstracts are used to screen, not to extract findings. The UK guide to quick scoping reviews and rapid evidence assessments [23] defines second-pass screening as "reading the abstract or first paragraph". It also says: "Assigning confidence in the statements can only be done from a REA or SR because understanding the robustness (quality) of the evidence is essential."

Two studies show why abstracts need care. Both are from biomedical research, so the transfer to policy research is my inference.

- Li and others reviewed 17 studies. "The level of inconsistency was reported to have a median of 39%". Major inconsistency had a median of 19% [24].
- Boutron and others studied 72 trials with non-significant main results. Spin appeared in the conclusions of the abstract in 58.3% [25].

## 6. Known failure modes

| Failure mode | What the source says | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Anchoring on a preferred option | Consider the service needed, "not from the perspective of a preconceived solution". "A predetermined or complete final option should be avoided." | [2, paras 2.11 and 4.29] |
| Weak objectives | "The strategic case for many proposals is weak." So longlisting "is fundamentally undermined". | [20, para 1.11] |
| Straw-man alternatives | "unrealistic straw men alternatives"; options added "only for the sake of having additional choices" | [8; 5, p. 117] |
| Nested options | Options built "like Russian dolls" make it "very difficult if not impossible" to reject the measures in option 1. "This introduces a bias." | [5, p. 122] |
| Clustering | A shortlist should avoid "clustering around a single preferred direction". | [7, s. 7.9] |
| Premature exclusion | "Do not exclude a priori options with little support or facing strong opposition." Options "should not be discounted just because they haven't been considered before". | [5, p. 117; 6] |
| Too few options | Three is the minimum. One option as a "fait accompli" is not acceptable. | [6] |
| Limited search | A study of more than 400 decisions names "limiting the search for remedies" as a trap. Secondary: university news release. | [26] |
| Optimism bias | "the proven tendency for appraisals to be over-optimistic about key assumptions" | [1, para 2.25] |
| False precision | A "spuriously accurate" ratio gives "a misleading impression of the degree of certainty". | [20, para 2.4] |
| Thresholds set after seeing scores | "may induce unforeseen bias" | [14, p. 116] |

## 7. Recommendations for our design

These are my recommendations. They are proposals for the owner to decide. Each lists its support.

1. **Exclude only on three grounds: boundary, authority, and clear irrelevance to the aim.** These match the sources' hard tests: stated limits, legal feasibility and relevance. Treat implementation and transferability as information on the card. *Support: [1, para 5.9; 5, pp. 118–119; 7, s. 6.12].*

2. **Require a "clearly fails" standard for every exclusion.** If the evidence is mixed or missing, keep the option and flag it. *Support: [3] ("clearly"); [5] ("self-evident and indisputable").*

3. **Let the user mark an implementation limit as hard.** A stated budget ceiling or deadline is a constraint in the sources. Exclude on it only when the user says it is a limit and the breach is clear. Otherwise show it as a flag. *Support: [2, para 4.12; 3; 14, p. 116] (thresholds set in advance).*

4. **Split authority into two tests.** "Outside your power" and "unlawful for anyone". Keep an option that another body could adopt visible, with the body named, even when it leaves the list. *Support: [5] (conferral; political feasibility caution).*

5. **Consider three additions to the constraint kinds: acceptability, timing, dependency.** The sources name all three. Acceptability should inform, not exclude, unless the user states it as a ruling. *Support: [2, paras 4.12–4.13; 1, para 5.11; 5].*

6. **Keep a visible list of discarded options with one reason each.** Every source that screens asks for this. *Support: [1, para 5.4; 5, p. 118; 2, para 2.12].*

7. **Cut to a shortlist on four tests, in this order.** (i) Passes the hard constraints. (ii) Serves the aim. (iii) Is distinct: merge or drop near-duplicates and clearly dominated options. (iv) Adds spread: different kinds of action and different levels of ambition, including a do minimum. Use rough merit (likely effect, cost band, feasibility, evidence strength) to choose within these tests. *Support: [1, paras 5.17–5.19; 3; 5 (identifiability); 7, s. 7.9; 14, s. 4.5].*

8. **Aim for about five shortlisted options, within a range of three to six, plus the baseline.** *Support: [1, para 5.17; 3; 6; 7, s. 7.9].*

9. **Do not use a weighted score for the cut.** Use a rating with a written reason and the evidence cited. *Support: [1, paras 5.15 and 5.23; 2, para 4.43].*

10. **Name a front runner only as "preferred way forward", and say it may change.** *Support: [1, para 5.20].*

11. **At the longlist stage, show an early signal per option, not a finding.** Show: direction of reported effect; a cost band; feasibility notes; evidence strength as its own field; where the evidence comes from. Use bands and ranges. Do not show a point estimate or a pooled effect. *Support: [1, para 5.10; 2, para 4.3; 15; 16; 21].*

12. **Label the signal by its source and stage.** For example: "Early signal, from abstracts. Not yet assessed." Keep observed and modelled outcomes apart. Keep "no evidence found" apart from "evidence of no effect". *Support: [23] (confidence needs a quality assessment); [24; 25] (abstracts mislead); [21] (maps do not synthesise).*

13. **Withhold the effect signal when evidence is thin.** Show "too little evidence to say" in place of a direction. *Support: [15, p. 7].*

14. **Leave effect size, cost estimates and the transferability judgement to the assessment stage.** The longlist may note where studies took place. The judgement on how far they apply belongs with full-text reading. *Support: [1, para 5.10; 4, footnote 30; 17] (moderators need review-level evidence).*

15. **Define an option at the grain of an approach type, not a named programme and not a package.** This matches the EEF, YEF and crime toolkits and the EC "measure". Packages can be built later. *Support: [5, p. 120; 15; 16; 17].*

16. **Guard against the failure modes in the list itself.** Avoid nested options. Do not add weak options to fill the count. Do not let the suggestions source crowd out the literature source, or the reverse. *Support: [5, pp. 117 and 122; 8].*
